## Key Question 1 — Carbohydrate, fluid and sodium targets by session duration and race distance

### Takeaway
The category converges on: no fuel needed under ~60 min; 30–60 g CHO/h for 1–2 h; push toward 90–120 g CHO/h (glucose:fructose mixes, trained gut) for races over 2 h; 16–32 oz (≈475–950 mL) fluid and 600–1,200 mg sodium per hour in the heat; rehydrate ~150 % of losses afterward. Daily intake targets are 3–12 g CHO/kg/day and 1.2–2.2 g protein/kg/day, scaling with load and age.

### Cited Findings

Carbohydrate during exercise, by duration
- "If you're racing for less than one hour, some water is probably all you need, if anything. If there's a lull in the racing, taking a gel at the 20-minute mark may be optimal." Between one and two hours: start well-fueled and take "top-up carbs" because intensity limits absorption. Over two hours: "consume as much carbohydrate as is tolerable for the duration of a race." — Tom Epton, [Coaching How to Break the 90g of Carbs Rule](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-break-90g-carbs-rule/)
- "Carbohydrate absorption becomes a limiting factor in some people at 60g per hour and above — or roughly three gels ... However, we know that many athletes can absorb well more than 100 grams per hour." — [Coaching How to Break the 90g of Carbs Rule](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-break-90g-carbs-rule/)
- "Recent research indicates that the ideal amount of carbohydrates to consume during these events [Ironman, stage racing, ultramarathons] for peak performance is 90-120g CHO/hour." Glucose/maltodextrin alone is capped at "~60 g·h-1"; adding fructose (different transporter, "2:1 ratio") lets absorption exceed that. Hydrogels are "more likely to be of use ... where the dose of carbohydrate ingested during exercise is notably high (> 90 g/hr)"; in a 2022 MSSE study (120 min at 70 % VO2max + 5 km TT, 90 g/h 2:1 glucose:fructose) the hydrogel improved performance by "~2.1%" vs the standard drink with less GI distress, while at 70 g/h a hydrogel gave no benefit. — Elizabeth Inpyn, [The Science of Sugars in Endurance Sports](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/sugars-in-endurance-sports/)
- Sports dietitian Briana Butler (quoted): "If you're training or racing for an hour or less at a moderate pace ... you probably don't need anything more than water with a little salt in it. You could also add in a carb rinse." "Once you go for much longer than an hour ... 30 to 60 grams per hour is a simple guideline ... I usually suggest breaking it into 15-minute increments." — Phil White, [How to Regulate Sugar Consumption Without Compromising Athlete Performance](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/regulate-sugar-without-compromising-performance/)
- "Consuming 30-60g carbohydrate during training can help to assist in maintaining blood glucose levels"; take "+/- 30g carbohydrate" within 15 min before a workout. — Dr Rick Kattouf, [Understanding Why Your Athletes Need More Carbs](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/your-athletes-need-more-carbs/)
- A durable athlete can "ingest (and absorb) a lot of carbohydrates (30-120g/hr depending on intensity, duration, and workout/race goal)." — Matti Rowe, [Predicting and Measuring Kilojoules to Determine Athlete Durability](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/predicting-measuring-kilojoules-trainingpeaks/)
- Worked race example: "an athlete consuming 80g of carbohydrates for three hours on a marathon would need to consume between three and four gels per hour, totalling between nine and 12 gels of roughly 25g each"; using on-course nutrition saves "up to 300g of weight." Professional run splits vary "by up to 15 minutes" course to course, which can be "the difference between an extra gel or not." — Tom Epton, [Make Big Gains Race Planning a Triathlon Using Science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/race-planning-triathlon-using-science/)

Gut-training protocol (how to reach >90 g/h)
- Baseline test: "Ride for race duration in zone one or two — not race distance — and attempt to consume 90g of carbs per hour ... Then pick up carbohydrate consumption in the last hour and a half to 100g/hour. If your athlete is a triathlete, have them run off the bike for 30 to 60 minutes after the ride, consuming carbohydrates at the same rate." Progression: "once a week, increasing the number of carbs per hour by five to 10% each week"; plateau typically "between week six and week eight," then add race pace. — [Coaching How to Break the 90g of Carbs Rule](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-break-90g-carbs-rule/)
- "From what we know about gut training, this can take as many as 10 sessions ... more likely to be once per week"; test on bricks of "a solid bike (i.e., 90 mins) and then ... a solid run (i.e., 90 mins)" because bricks are where GI issues occur; "Often, there is underconsumption of carbohydrates in training during race-paced sessions." — Scott Tindal, [Breaking Down Nutrition Plans for Athletes With Three Simple Questions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/nutrition-plans-athletes-three-questions/)
- Gut training "has been shown to improve carbohydrate malabsorption, improve glucose availability in the blood, and substantially reduce gut discomfort." — [The Science of Sugars in Endurance Sports](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/sugars-in-endurance-sports/)

Daily / pre / post carbohydrate and protein
- USADA-derived: "3-12g carbohydrates per kilogram of body weight all throughout the day"; macro split "50% Carbohydrate, 25% Protein, 25% Fat at each meal/snack"; pre-workout "three hours prior ... 1-2g carbohydrate per kilogram"; post-workout "1-1.5g carbohydrate per kilogram" immediately, with glucose uptake enhanced "within 30 minutes or so after exercise." — [Understanding Why Your Athletes Need More Carbs](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/your-athletes-need-more-carbs/)
- Key-workout loading: "10-12g/carbs/kg/lean body mass for up to 48 hours prior to a key workout or event"; "1-4g/carbs/kg/lean body mass/hr for 1-4hrs prior"; "Reduce fat, fiber, and protein 24 hours prior"; after intense or ≥90 min exercise "1.2+g/carbs/kg/lean body mass/hr for 4-6 hours" plus ".2-.5g/protein/kg/lean body mass/hr for the remainder of the day." — Taylor Thomas, [Pre and Post Workout Fueling for Your Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/pre-and-post-workout-fueling-for-your-athletes/)
- Protein: "1.6-2.0g/kg body weight per day, and up to 2.2g/kg per day for older athletes"; plant-based athletes must prioritise leucine sources; calcium target "1000mg/day"; vitamin D3 "1000-5000IU/day" after a confirmed deficiency. — Elizabeth Inpyn, [Optimizing Nutrition for Endurance Athletes Who Use a Plant-Based Diet](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/optimizing-nutrition-athletes-plant-based-diet/)
- Masters (35+): protein "~1.2-2.0g/kg/day ... slightly higher on heavy training or strength training days (~50% increase)"; "~30g of protein per meal and 10-20g of protein when snacking"; sarcopenia loses "~1% per year" muscle mass and "~3% per year" strength; creatine "3-5g/d"; omega-3 "3 to 5 g/d"; carbohydrate recommendations "are generally the same for all athletes" but must fit "a lower energy budget." — Elizabeth Inpyn, [Addressing the Changing Nutrition Needs of Masters Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/addressing-nutrition-needs-masters-athletes/)
- Energy expenditure accounting: for cycling "there's a 1:1 relationship between kJ and calories (kcal) because the conversion factors from kJ to Calories (4.184) and human efficiency (20-25%, or ~.225) cancel each other out" (500 kJ ≈ 531 kcal). Durability field test: 5-min max, ride to 2,000 kJ, repeat; "300 → 270 W = 10% reduction." — [Predicting and Measuring Kilojoules to Determine Athlete Durability](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/predicting-measuring-kilojoules-trainingpeaks/)

Fluid and sodium
- Racing in heat: "most athletes should be able to consume 16 to 32 ounces of fluid per hour, along with 600 to 1,200 mg of sodium." Fluids with electrolytes "at a similar rate to fluid loss ... should be viewed as essential during workouts lasting 90 minutes or more." In races "under 90 minutes" absorption is worse, so "drink slightly less." Off the bike/run "drink to thirst"; urine should be "straw-colored — not deep yellow ... and not perfectly clear." — Conrad Goeringer, [How to Train and Race in Hot Conditions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-train-and-race-in-hot-conditions/)
- Post-exercise: Chilean study (14 male college athletes dehydrated 3.5 % body mass in 32 °C) — rehydrating with 1.5× fluid lost restored HRV; recommendation "consume approximately 150 percent of the fluid lost during exercise ... and to use electrolyte supplements for extended workouts." — Simon Wegerif, [The Whole Picture: Nutrition](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/total-load-nutrition/)
- Supplementary (not Science tag): "aim to drink 500 mL every 30-45 minutes in hot and humid conditions" but "drink to thirst ... don't force fluid" to avoid hyponatremia. — Zach Nehr, [Heat Training for Cyclists](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/heat-training-for-cyclists/)
- Daily baseline for older athletes: coach Lawrence Herrera recommends "at least an ounce of water for every kilogram of bodyweight, adding more depending on their training duration and intensity and the heat and humidity level"; night cramps in new 60+ athletes are attributed to "not drinking enough, consuming too little sodium and other electrolytes, or both"; thirst mechanism declines with age so "drink-to-thirst ... is likely insufficient." — Phil White, [The Art of Coaching 60+ Athletes in Endurance Sports](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-athletes-in-60s-endurance-sports/)
- Winter: sweat rates are lower, athletes under-drink; prescribe "a timed or distance-based schedule (such as take a gulp every 10 minutes or half-mile)." — Phil White, [5 Things Your Athletes Need to Improve Their Winter Trail Running](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/5-things-your-athletes-need-to-improve-their-winter-trail-running/)
- Alcohol: dilute to "three percent" effective concentration (a pint of water per pint of beer, three glasses of water per glass of wine); HRV falls "about 10 percent" at twice the recommended 2 drinks/day and "25 percent" at four times; glycogen resynthesis takes "nearly twice as long"; abstain "three to four days" before a competition. — [The Whole Picture: Nutrition](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/total-load-nutrition/)

Sweat testing
- "Measurements of fluid need through pre- and post-training and competition weights are recommended to help determine fluid requirements"; Fuelin "recommends that all athletes do this regularly before a competition." — [Addressing the Changing Nutrition Needs of Masters Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/addressing-nutrition-needs-masters-athletes/)
- A performance-focused athlete's weekly action item may be to "perform a sweat test to understand their hydration requirements." — [Breaking Down Nutrition Plans for Athletes With Three Simple Questions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/nutrition-plans-athletes-three-questions/)
- "A single sweat test is rarely enough because sweat rate and sweat sodium can shift meaningfully with context." Sweat sodium concentration rises with sweat rate ("faster sweat flow leaves less time for sodium to be reabsorbed"). Drivers of day-to-day variability: "exercise intensity, ambient temperature, solar load, humidity (which impairs evaporation and can push sweat rate higher), wind (which increases convective and evaporative cooling and can reduce sweat needs), clothing ... hydration status, heat acclimation, aerobic fitness." A resting pilocarpine test "overestimated low-intensity exercise [Na+], aligned best at moderate intensity, and underestimated at high intensity." Method: "use sweat rate to set a practical fluid target per hour and total sodium loss to estimate sodium per hour needs, then validate across multiple representative sessions"; compare "3-minute slopes" of sweat/sodium loss against power and HR. — TrainingPeaks, [Analyze 360: See and Analyze Every Metric in Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)

Caffeine
- "3 to 6 mg/kg [of bodyweight], approximately 60 min prior to exercise" (2019 Nutrients review); worked examples "175-pound client ... between 240 and 480 milligrams", "110-pound athlete ... 150 and 300 milligrams"; 8-oz coffee ≈ 95 mg, double espresso ≈ 80 mg, 8-oz black tea ≈ 47 mg, dark chocolate ≈ 12 mg/oz. Performance: 5 km time improved "1.1%" in well-trained and "one percent" in recreational runners; caffeine+carbs before a cycling TT improved performance "by 9% compared to water alone and by 6% compared to the carb loaders." Start at the low end; excess causes GI distress, palpitations, anxiety; "there's no evidence-based reason to suggest that any of your athletes cycle on and off caffeine." — Phil White, [How Endurance Athletes Can Use Caffeine More Effectively](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-endurance-athletes-can-use-caffeine-more-effectively/)

Fasted training and keto (how they modify targets)
- Fasted evening cyclists (30 min at ~60 % VO2max + 15-min TT) were "3.8% worse" than fed; fasted sprint-interval riders had lower peak power and total work; 10 days of intermittent fasting reduced time-to-exhaustion and power; literature review: "no benefit to athletic performance while fasting." Practical rule: fast only for "low intensity ... a tempo run at a steady clip or a recovery ride"; fuel before intervals, hills, sprints or race-pace long sessions. — Phil White, [Intermittent Fasting's Effects On Endurance Results And Health](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/intermittent-fasting-effects-endurance-results/)
- "During fasted periods, perform easier, shorter efforts. When fed, perform higher intensity and longer efforts"; most find "18-20 hours per day" protocols most practical; adherence is hardest in "the first two weeks." — Dr Jeff Sankoff, [Intermittent Fasting for Athletes: What Does the Science Say?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/intermittent-fasting-for-athletes-what-does-the-science-say/)
- Keto = "under 30 grams" CHO/day, "five to 10 percent carbs, 30 to 35 percent protein, and 55 to 60 percent fat"; research "conflicting" — equal 100 km TT performance in one 12-week study, but other work found it "harder ... to complete high-intensity training sessions." — Phil White, [Does the Keto Diet Work for Endurance Athletes?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/does-the-keto-diet-work-for-endurance-athletes/)

### Inferences
- A duration-tiered rule the assistant can apply: <60 min → water (± mouth rinse); 60–120 min → 30–60 g CHO/h; >120 min → 60–90 g/h minimum, 90–120 g/h for trained guts using glucose:fructose; ultra/Ironman planning at 80 g/h ≈ 3–4 × 25 g gels per hour. Flag any logged race >2 h with reported intake <60 g/h as under-fuelled by the category's own standard.
- Hydration flag: in hot races, logged intake below ~475 mL/h or sodium below ~600 mg/h falls under the category's floor; in sub-90-min events, lower intake is expected and should not be flagged.
- Sweat-rate estimation from pre/post body mass is the category's baseline method; the assistant should treat a single test as one data point and re-test across conditions (hot/cool, easy/hard, indoor/outdoor).
- Caffeine dosing can be computed directly from body mass (3–6 mg/kg) with a ~60-min lead.

### Gaps
- No article gives fluid or sodium targets scaled to body mass or measured sweat rate (e.g., mL/kg/h or mg/L); only the generic 16–32 oz/h and 600–1,200 mg/h ranges appear.
- No category article gives a step-by-step pre/post weighing protocol (correcting for intake and urine); the method is only named.
- No sodium concentration reference ranges (e.g., "salty sweater" thresholds) are given.
- No race-distance-specific carbohydrate table (sprint/Olympic/70.3/140.6) beyond the duration tiers above.

## Key Question 2 — Heat acclimation protocol, timeline, and quantified performance/HR effects of heat

### Takeaway
The category recommends 10–14 days of progressive heat exposure (or 10–15 post-exercise sauna/hot-bath sessions of 20–30 min at ~40 °C / 104 °F over roughly two weeks, three sessions per week as a minimum), maintenance of 1–2 sessions per week if the block ends more than two weeks before the race, and it quantifies heat effects mainly through HR–power decoupling (target ≤5 %; ~8 % at Heat Strain Index 3.8 in a well-adapted athlete) and, in the supplementary article, a 16 % VO2max drop per +1 °C core temperature.

### Cited Findings

Protocols and timelines
- Classic studies used "an hour a day or more for ten to 14 days, six or seven days a week"; practical hybrid: normal training then "three times a week, get in a sauna or hot tub for 20 to 30 minutes afterward. Continue this for two weeks, building up the amount of time." Core temperature target "ideally somewhere around 101 degrees" (F; ≈38.3 °C). Taper: if the block ends "more than two weeks before a competition ... incorporating some maintenance, like doing a heat session once or twice a week." Heat-acclimated runners "still got a lot faster even when testing in cooler conditions" (Minson 2010). Alex Hutchinson interviewed by Phil White, [Everything Coaches Need to Know About Heat Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/everything-coaches-need-to-know-about-heat-training/)
- "Typically, it takes about 10 to 14 days of training in hot weather to incite measurable adaptations ... Starting with short, easy sessions of 30 minutes and gradually increasing the duration by ten or so minutes per day"; sauna alternative "10 to 15 sessions lasting 20 to 30 minutes directly after exercise is sufficient for improving heat tolerance and boosting endurance" (Scoon 2006; Stanley 2014). Body's functional core range "98.6 to 103 degrees Fahrenheit or 37 to 39.5 degrees Celsius." — Conrad Goeringer, [How to Train and Race in Hot Conditions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-train-and-race-in-hot-conditions/)
- Safety limits: Finnish sauna study participants averaged "just 17 minutes per session"; "won't derive much, if any, additional advantages from staying in a sauna, hot tub, or bath for longer than 20 minutes"; hot baths at "104 degrees" (F) gave benefits "similar ... to an hour of cycling" (Loughborough); stop when "woozy, confused, or upset" or "excessively thirsty"; frequency "four to seven times per week" was associated with lowest mortality in a 20-year cohort of 2,300 men (31 % vs 49 % once-weekly); rehydrate with sodium ("a glass of water with a pinch of sea salt"). — Phil White, [Help Your Athletes Heat Train Safely](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/help-your-athletes-heat-train-safely/)
- Post-exercise hot-water immersion at "104 degrees Fahrenheit" after a 40-min steady run acclimated athletes better than 40 min treadmill running at 91 °F or 60 min at 66 °F (both ~65 % VO2max; 27 participants; McIntyre 2021). Pre-race: "cooling vests until right before the starter's pistol"; cold drink before a TT extended time to exhaustion by "almost 12 minutes" (Lee 2008). Dehydrated trail runners (17 subjects) were "measurably slower." Heat stroke: "immediate immersion in an ice bath for 30 minutes ... Immediate cooling leads to a 100% survival rate." Warning signs to back off then stop: "lightheaded, dizzy or nauseous." Arrive at the event site "a few days before." — Phil White, [How to Help Your Athletes Avoid Heat Stroke, Exhaustion and Syncope](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/help-athletes-avoid-heat-stroke/)
- Heat session definition (CORE sensor): "Heat sessions are performed between Heat Strain Index 3.0 and 6.9 ... A heat session generally sees a core temp higher than 38.0°C/100.4°F and a skin temp higher than 35°C/95°F." Troubleshooting: core <38.0 °C → raise power/pace; skin <35 °C → add clothing; put waterproof layer under thermal layer. — [Analyze 360: See and Analyze Every Metric in Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)
- Supplementary (not Science tag): "just 10 days of heat acclimatization improved time trial performance, power output, plasma volume, and maximal cardiac output in both hot and cool conditions" (Lorenzo 2010); "Most studies find that 9-12 days ... is the minimum"; prescription "easy sessions (~50% VO2max) lasting 60-90 minutes in 104°F (40°C) conditions"; 2019 meta-analysis ranked mitigation strategies: aerobic fitness > heat acclimation > pre-cooling > fluid ingestion (Alhadad 2019). — Zach Nehr, [Heat Training for Cyclists](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/heat-training-for-cyclists/)

Quantified effects of heat on HR, power, and core temperature
- Mechanism: "Only ~20% of the calories you burn go toward muscular power ... when you pedal at 200 watts, you're also creating 800 watts of heat." Hot skin diverts blood to the periphery "and causes heart rate to increase. This is heat stress, and it causes performance loss." Heat Zones: HSI 0–0.9 no strain; HSI 1.0–2.9 "Heart rate is starting to decouple from power/pace"; HSI 3.0–6.9 "heart rate has substantially decoupled from power/pace. Performance and exercise capacity are substantially reduced"; HSI ≥7.0 risk of heat illness. Worked example: on a 6:30 h ride HSI reached 3.8 after an hour of climbing with "Power-to-heart-rate decoupling ... about 8%" in a "very well heat-adapted" athlete. Decoupling benchmark: "For longer events, most consider 5% the maximum amount of decoupling for optimal performance." Heat-threshold test: 15 min Z2 with fans → remove fans, add waterproof + thermal layers, hold the HR while reducing power; stop when power/pace has dropped "15–20%"; read off the HSI at each decoupling level. — [Analyze 360: See and Analyze Every Metric in Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)
- "Anyone who has exercised for more than a couple of hours in a warm environment may have noticed that their heart rate continued to increase despite remaining at the same pace or power. Joe Friel describes this phenomenon as 'heart rate decoupling,' and one of its causes is reduced stroke volume of the heart brought on by dehydration." — [The Whole Picture: Nutrition](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/total-load-nutrition/)
- Racing: "rely more heavily on rated perceived exertion and heart rate within the race, both of which will be higher than on mild days"; "The single most important adjustment ... is to lower the target effort"; "the combination of heat and humidity is worse than just heat." — [How to Train and Race in Hot Conditions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-train-and-race-in-hot-conditions/)
- Coach usage: "We also closely monitor Pa:HR to assess cardiac drift, which offers insight into durability, heat stress, and fueling or hydration issues." — Molly Seidel's coach interview, [Fatigue Resistance in Ultras](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/fatigue-resistance-ultras-molly-seidel-training/)
- Supplementary (not Science tag): "Just a 1°C increase in core body temperature (i.e., hyperthermia) caused a 16% reduction in VO2max, in addition to ... a significantly elevated maximal heart rate" (Nybo 2001, trained cyclists); "cyclists can experience a 4-5°C increase in core body temperature during a sub-hour time trial effort in the heat"; "even at an easy Zone 1 power output, your heart rate and RPE will climb into Zone 2, then Zone 3, and maybe even Zone 4" during heat sessions. — [Heat Training for Cyclists](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/heat-training-for-cyclists/)
- Sodium-bicarbonate skin cream applied 30–60 min before heat exposure "could offset the increase in heart rate and core body temperature caused by a combination of heat and dehydration" (Korey Stringer Institute; industry-linked source). TrainingPeaks' Beta Analyze can plot core body temperature from .fit files. — [How to Help Your Athletes Avoid Heat Stroke, Exhaustion and Syncope](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/help-athletes-avoid-heat-stroke/)
- GI red flag in heat: "GI issues are strongly correlated with uncommon levels of exertion and/or heat ... those who are acclimated to the conditions are much less likely to experience GI distress"; muscle tissue can exceed ">100 F ... @3cm depth"; regular emergency evacuation means "the effort needs to be dialed back"; avoid NSAIDs/aspirin. — Patrick Smith, [Pooping & Puking](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/pooping-puking-why-extreme-exertion-leads-to-surprising-evacuation/)

### Inferences
- Acclimation rule: schedule ≥10 (ideally 14) consecutive exposure days, or ≥10–15 post-session 20–30 min hot baths/saunas over ~2 weeks, ending with maintenance 1–2×/week if more than 14 days separate the block from race day. Arrival 3+ days before a hot race is additive.
- Session interpretation rule: HR–power/pace decoupling >5 % in a long session is the category's threshold for "heat/hydration cost"; ~8 % corresponds to HSI ≈3.8 even in a well-adapted athlete, so decoupling of 8–10 % in an unadapted athlete on a hot day is expected, not a fitness failure. Decoupling from dehydration typically emerges after ~2 h.
- Heat-day logs should be evaluated on HR/RPE not power/pace; a heat session that keeps Z1 power but shows Z2–Z4 HR is functioning as intended, not a sign of poor fitness.
- Core temperature ≥38.0 °C with skin ≥35 °C is the operational definition of a "counted" heat session for athletes with a CORE sensor.

### Gaps
- No article in the category (nor the supplement) gives a per-degree ambient temperature penalty on HR, pace or power (e.g., "+X bpm per °C" or "-Y % pace above 20 °C"). The only per-degree figure is the core-temperature one (16 % VO2max per +1 °C core), from outside the tag.
- No humidity- or WBGT-indexed adjustment table appears anywhere in the category.
- The rate of decay of heat adaptations is explicitly described as "contested and uncertain" (Hutchinson).
- No article specifies acclimation days differently for women, masters or unfit athletes.

## Key Question 3 — How power/pace targets should be adjusted for heat, humidity, wind, cold, air quality and altitude

### Takeaway
The category offers qualitative-plus-threshold guidance: in heat lower the target effort and pace by HR/RPE with ≤5 % decoupling as the ceiling; humidity worsens heat because it blocks evaporation; aerodynamic drag scales with velocity squared (wind matters more at higher speed, drafting saves ~2 % of marathon energy); in cold, warm up longer and keep drinking; on smoky days AQI >200 means easy/indoor/short and AQI >300 means rest. Altitude is barely covered — only as a training analogue to heat/humidity, a hint of "power loss" at altitude, and a ferritin ≥50 µg/L prerequisite.

### Cited Findings

Heat and humidity
- "Blindly adhering to pacing goals is a recipe for disaster ... athletes should rely more heavily on rated perceived exertion and heart rate ... peel back the pace." "In high humidity, the moisture in the air prevents the evaporation of liquid ... it becomes even more important to adjust pace." "It is also important to reign in intensity on hot days." Cooling on course: ice in jersey/cap, cold sponges on chest, "well worth slowing down at aid stations." — [How to Train and Race in Hot Conditions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-train-and-race-in-hot-conditions/)
- Decoupling ceiling for pacing: "most consider 5% the maximum amount of decoupling for optimal performance" in long events; cooling skin by "dousing with water bottles" kept HSI low on a later climb even as core rose. — [Analyze 360](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)
- Symptom-based rule: "first back off their pace if necessary and then, if the warning signs don't decrease or progress further, stop and seek help." — [How to Help Your Athletes Avoid Heat Stroke, Exhaustion and Syncope](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/help-athletes-avoid-heat-stroke/)
- Supplementary (not Science tag): heat "causes ... an increase in heart rate, core body temperature, skin temperature, sweat rate, and blood lactate"; "as the body heats up, power output goes down"; a hot ride in August feels easier than June because of passive acclimation. — [Heat Training for Cyclists](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/heat-training-for-cyclists/)

Wind and aerodynamics
- Cycling: "the force of drag is proportional to the square of your speed ... as your speed doubles, the drag increases four times"; "Riding at exactly the same power is only the most efficient use of your energy on a completely flat, windless, perfectly surfaced road." — Tom Epton, [Understanding the Physics of Cycling](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-physics-of-cycling/)
- Variable-power pacing: "From 20kph on the climb to 80kph on a descent, drag force will increase 16 times"; doubling power on a descent raises speed "only ... about 40%"; put watts into climbs/headwind sections and recover on descents (BestBikeSplit logic). — [Make Big Gains Race Planning a Triathlon Using Science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/race-planning-triathlon-using-science/)
- Toy model P = ½ρu³CdA + 30 W (rolling 20 W + drivetrain 10 W): at sea-level air density, CdA 0.3, 35 km/h → ≈200 W; air density ρ is "an example of environmental conditions making a considerable difference in our bike speed. It's why many hour record attempts happen at altitude, despite the power loss seen there"; 5 % CdA reduction (0.40→0.38) from a skinsuit, 25 % (0.38→0.29) from a TT bike. — Tom Epton, [What's Important: Aerodynamics or Power?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/whats-important-aerodynamics-power/)
- Running: "roughly 2% of a marathon runner's energy goes to overcoming air resistance. This could convert to up to three and a half minutes for a three hour marathon runner"; air resistance "increases with the square of velocity"; "running in a pack is the best way to avoid the wind." — Tom Epton, [Understanding the Physics of Running for Triathlon Coaches](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/physics-running-triathlon-coaches/)
- Open-water chop: if waves ruin 1 in 5 strokes, velocity falls 20 %; shortening stroke 10 % and raising rate 10 % recovers half; goggle lens by light (dark for sun, yellow/amber for overcast). — [Make Big Gains Race Planning a Triathlon Using Science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/race-planning-triathlon-using-science/)

Cold
- Winter running: "it's going to take your athletes longer to get their muscles, ligaments, and tendons ready"; do a dynamic warm-up with ankle mobility and bounding, and "run slower for longer than usual" at the start; protect head, hands, feet; the "40 to 45 percent" heat-loss-through-head claim is debunked but head cover still matters; hydration schedule as above. — [5 Things Your Athletes Need to Improve Their Winter Trail Running](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/5-things-your-athletes-need-to-improve-their-winter-trail-running/)
- Cold water: pools run "high 60s to low 80s Fahrenheit (16-27 Celsius)" while open water "may never reach the high 60s"; "windchill and high humidity amplify cold effects"; enter slowly, exit if "brain freeze or runaway breathing persist"; "Swimming in cold water burns more calories" — refuel with simple sugars and warm (not hot) drinks; "Hypothermia kills." — Patrick Smith, [Surviving Late-Season Open Water Swimming](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/surviving-late-season-open-water-swimming/)
- Wetsuits: "3/2 mm" = 3 mm core, 2 mm limbs; "5/4/3" = 5 mm core, 4 mm legs, 3 mm arms; thermal linings and no water flushing are key; recognise hypothermia signs, never swim alone. — Carrie McCusker, [Choosing the Right Cold Water Wetsuit for Triathletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/cold-water-wetsuit-triathlon/)
- Cold-water immersion causes central blood redistribution; "The effect is worse in colder water and especially during exertion" (swimming-induced pulmonary oedema); two-thirds of 135 triathlon deaths/arrests (1985–2016, >5 M participants) occurred in the swim; per-event death risk "1.7 per 100,000" for triathlon vs "one per 100,000" for marathon; advice: "go easy at the beginning and ease into race pace." — Dr Jeff Sankoff, [Athlete Deaths in Triathlon and How to Prevent Them](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/athlete-deaths-in-triathlon-and-how-to-prevent-them/)
- Recovery cold immersion: effective protocols require "5-10 degrees celsius and 20-30 minutes"; performance benefit "inconclusive or ... no better than placebo" and cooling is "detrimental to muscle cell function ... in cases of anaerobic sprinting." — Dr Jeff Sankoff, [Does Cold Immersion Therapy Actually Give Athletes an Edge?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/does-cold-immersion-therapy-actually-give-athletes-an-edge/)

Air quality
- AQI thresholds: ">100 ... athletes with breathing considerations should limit outdoor activity"; "under 200 is ok for reasonably strenuous training outdoors for shorter durations"; "Above 200, even healthy athletes should dial it back to indoor, easier effort, and short duration"; "Above 300 means take a rest day." Low-risk 24-h PM10 "<150µg/m3", PM2.5 "35µg/m3"; "A highly exerted athlete moves up to five times their normal volume of air"; indoor training helps for PM10 but "not viable for PM2.5"; N95 filters particles not gases. — Patrick Smith, [Training Through Bad Air Quality](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/training-through-bad-air-quality/)

Altitude
- "The studies Larsen looked at showed that the body has similar reactions to running at altitude as it does to running in high humidity. So running at altitude really helped Meb and his other athletes prepare to perform in high heat and humidity"; conversely, heat-only training "wouldn't have created more red blood cells." — Matthew Futterman quoted in [How to Help Your Athletes Avoid Heat Stroke, Exhaustion and Syncope](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/help-athletes-avoid-heat-stroke/)
- Hutchinson compares heat training to altitude as the two main environmental stimuli, noting plasma-volume gains from heat versus red-cell focus at altitude; "for every factor you tweak, there will be both benefits and costs." — [Everything Coaches Need to Know About Heat Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/everything-coaches-need-to-know-about-heat-training/)
- Blood prerequisite: "a ferritin value of 50 mcg/l should be attained in athletes before altitude training, as iron demands in these situations are increased" (Swiss Medical Weekly); "Somewhere between 30 and 40 ng/mL should be sufficient for your athletes who don't live or train at altitude"; re-test blood when "making a big change in their environment, like going into high altitude." — Phil White, [Which Blood Tests Should Coaches Recommend?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaches-blood-testing-endurance-training/)
- "A well-rested and prepared athlete, with a combination of altitude, may find their [red cell] count higher than usual." — Jakub Novak, [The 4 Signs of Overtraining](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- Hour records happen at altitude "despite the power loss seen there." — [What's Important: Aerodynamics or Power?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/whats-important-aerodynamics-power/)
- Respiratory-muscle training masks improve inspiratory strength and lower dyspnoea; the article does not claim altitude simulation. — Dr Rick Kattouf, [How Masked Training Can Make You More Fit](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-masked-training-can-make-you-more-fit/)

### Inferences
- Heat/humidity modifier: cap prescribed intensity by HR/RPE rather than power/pace on hot days; treat a hot-day power/pace shortfall with ≤5 % decoupling as environment, and >5 % (or >8 % in an adapted athlete) as a fueling/hydration/heat problem worth annotating.
- Wind modifier: because drag ∝ v², headwind/tailwind splits should be judged on power (bike) or effort (run), not speed; a 2 % energy allowance for wind is reasonable for solo marathon-pace running, more at faster speeds.
- Cold modifier: expect and allow a longer warm-up (slower first km/min) and do not interpret low sweat/low intake as adequate hydration.
- Air-quality modifier: AQI 100–200 → shorten strenuous outdoor sessions; 200–300 → move indoors, easy, short; >300 → rest/relocate.
- Altitude: the category gives no quantitative adjustment; the assistant should not apply an altitude pace/power correction from these sources alone, but can flag a pre-camp ferritin check (≥50 µg/L).

### Gaps
- No altitude-specific article exists in the Science category: no acclimatisation timeline, no power/VO2max penalty per 1,000 m, no live-high/train-low guidance.
- No numeric humidity or dew-point adjustment; no cold-temperature pace/HR adjustment; no wind-speed-to-watts table beyond the v² relationship.
- The 2 % marathon wind figure is for still-air self-generated drag, not for a given headwind speed.

## Key Question 4 — Signs of low energy availability (LEA/RED-S) or poor fueling that coaches should flag in athletes' data

### Takeaway
The category's red flags are: unexplained rising resting HR (≥5 bpm above baseline, especially for weeks), falling HRV, HR/effort decoupling on well-executed sessions, declining performance on previously attainable efforts, recurrent or slow-healing injuries and bone stress injuries, menstrual irregularity or amenorrhea, frequent illness, cramps/dehydration complaints, weight loss with food fixation, and low ferritin/vitamin D/B12 on bloods; treatment starts with +300–600 kcal/day and a multidisciplinary team.

### Cited Findings

Physiological and data signs
- Female Athlete Triad physical signs: "significant weight loss, frequent complaints of dehydration, muscle cramps, weakness, and fatigue"; psychological: "complaining of feeling fat despite being thin, anxiety and/or depression, difficulty concentrating, and preoccupation with weight and eating"; later: "amenorrhea and, eventually, stress fractures"; "Any female athlete who sustains a stress fracture should be encouraged to see their physician for a comprehensive evaluation of their menstrual health." Restoring energy balance "is almost always adequate to reverse the menstrual irregularities." Author notes RED-S (2014 IOC) extension to men "remains a topic of significant controversy." — Dr Jeff Sankoff, [How to Prevent The Female Athlete Triad as a Coach](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-prevent-the-female-athlete-triad-as-a-coach/)
- Eating-disorder specialist Dr Margherite Mascolo: "If an athlete begins to develop injuries more frequently than would otherwise be expected and seems to be taking longer to recover from them, this suggests poor nutritional intake"; other signs: "losing the ability to hit previously attainable training efforts," "use of weight loss aids like laxatives or diuretics," changes in menstrual regularity. Severity assessed with the "RED-S CAT"; best outcomes with "a dietician, physician and therapist." — Dr Jeff Sankoff, [Coaching Endurance Athletes with Eating Disorders](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-endurance-athletes-with-eating-disorders/)
- Pre-workout under-fueling in the log: a hard VO2max session done "in a fasted or glycogen deficient state" can produce "decreased performance, heightened RPE, higher heart rate, and leave the athlete feeling defeated"; track next-day feel, sleep, motivation, fatigue as "softer" indicators of poor post-workout fueling. — [Pre and Post Workout Fueling for Your Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/pre-and-post-workout-fueling-for-your-athletes/)
- Resting HR: "Usually, an RHR 5 bpm above their average indicates they need more recovery time. However, if their resting heart rate doesn't stabilize and remains elevated for multiple weeks, then it could mean they're overtrained or even developing an illness"; overtrained athletes show "a decreased count of red blood cells, hemoglobin and hematocrit"; HRV "lower than usual, for a longer period of time, despite taking recovery days"; plateau — "For most amateur athletes, this point starts around 110 CTL." — Jakub Novak, [The 4 Signs of Overtraining](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- Symptoms that should trigger bloodwork: "sleep disruption, trouble reaching and maintaining paces, higher resting and working heart rate, and increased perception of fatigue." Ferritin: clinical range "10 – 120 ng/mL for females and 20 – 250 ng/mL for males" but "performance limitations in athletes whose ferritin is below 25 ng/mL"; "30 and 40 ng/mL should be sufficient" (50 before altitude); ferritin can be "artificially high if an athlete is overtrained, sick, stressed or under-recovered," so pair with TIBC, serum iron, transferrin, CBC. Vitamin D <20 ng/mL → "12% more likely to suffer a stress fracture" than ≥40; supplement "2,000 IUs of vitamin D3" daily. B12 target "400-700 pg/mL." Test yearly, or every 3–6 months with deficiencies, ramping load or moving to altitude. — Phil White, [Which Blood Tests Should Coaches Recommend?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaches-blood-testing-endurance-training/)
- Ferritin action thresholds: "<30-35 ng/mL, then Iron deficiency treatment should be discussed, and if it's 35-60 ng/mL, then increasing iron may be a prudent step"; "If an athlete is suffering from decreased energy levels or poor recovery for more than seven days, it may be time to think about scheduling a blood test"; heme iron absorbed "up to 25%," non-heme "3-15%"; coffee, tea, alcohol impair absorption. — Taylor Thomas, [Iron Deficiency, Anemia and Endurance Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/iron-deficiency-anemia-and-endurance-athletes/)
- End-of-season: "Many athletes finish the season on a spectrum between feeling a bit flat to suffering from low energy availability"; RED-S "can't be diagnosed with a blood test alone"; symptoms "disrupted reproductive health, bone density and health problems, a reduction in immune function and a reduction in metabolism"; iron-deficiency symptoms "overlap with those of overtraining"; track HRV for "three months" before using it for decisions. — Tom Epton, [Determining When Athletes Should Train Again](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/determining-when-athletes-train-again/)
- Prevalence and treatment: "over 40% (44.1%) of female ultra-marathon athletes were found to be 'at risk', with 32% demonstrating DE behaviors ... 92.5% of the athletes lacked awareness of the TRIAD"; IOC: "male endurance athletes in running and non-weight bearing sports such as cycling, are at high risk for low BMD"; treatment "increases current EI by ∼300–600 kcal/day (1.2–2.4 MJ/day)" and fixes energy spread around sessions. — Phil White, [Lifestyle Factors That Add to Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lifestyle-factors-adding-training-load/)
- Compulsive training as a flag: among 202 female endurance athletes training ≥5 days/week, "23% tended to go too far in their workouts, 21% were at risk of disordered eating and a whopping 65% were in the LEA danger zone"; LEA risk "2.5 times" with disordered eating and ">5.5 times" with disordered eating plus exercise dependence; "view obsessive overtraining as a major red flag" and cross-check workout logs against a food journal. — Phil White, [How Training Addiction and Disordered Eating are Connected](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/training-addiction-disordered-eating-connected/)
- CGM as a fueling detector: "many athletes using CGM report that they have noticed they are underfueling"; "Declining glucose levels, low glucose, or periods with unexplained but sizable reductions in glucose probably reflect suboptimal glucose availability"; glucose is flat below the first threshold and rises with intensity; "overnight glucose levels may be a canary in the coal mine for a mismatch between training load and caloric intake" (early, unproven). — David Lipman, [Understanding How Continuous Blood Glucose Monitors Can Help Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/continuous-blood-glucose-monitor-athletes/)
- CGM accuracy "9.7% over 14 days" (Freestyle Libre) vs finger-prick 5–10 %; "care should be taken with athletes who have had a history of eating disorders" when asking for food diaries. — Tom Epton, [Lessons Learned Using a CGM to Optimize Nutrition & Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lessons-using-cgm-optimize-nutrition/)
- Menstrual tracking: "a shorter or non-existent period or Relative Energy Deficiency Syndrome ... is a tell-tale sign of overtraining. A lack of period can lead to injuries, decreased bone density"; cycles vary "25-40 days." — Mackenzie Madison with Dr Stacy Sims, [The Performance Advantages of Tracking Menstrual Cycles](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-performance-advantages-of-tracking-menstrual-cycles-with-dr-stacy-sims/)
- Bone: bone stress injuries occur at "roughly twenty percent per year in runners"; LEA "can significantly increase the risk of a bone stress injury"; chronic dehydration correlated with osteopenia in jockeys (confounded by energy deficit); complete remineralisation "up to a year." — Tom Epton, [The Connection Between Hydration & Bone Stress Injuries](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hydration-bone-stress-injuries/)
- Weight-loss context: "Weight becomes important as the race course tilts upward"; on flat/rolling courses "the energy deficit required for weight loss probably isn't worth the risk"; "Chronic energy expenditure ability is the training ceiling of any athlete." — Tom Epton, [Weight Loss for Endurance Athletes: Is It Worth the Risk?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/weight-loss-endurance-athletes/)
- Weight-loss phase expectations: a deliberate deficit brings "hunger, feelings of tiredness and sometimes poor performance during a session" and the athlete "is unlikely to smash their FTP"; "not for a chronic duration as that can lead to ... RED-s"; food diary (e.g., Lose It) for 7 days as a starting action. — Scott Tindal, [Breaking Down Nutrition Plans for Athletes With Three Simple Questions](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/nutrition-plans-athletes-three-questions/)
- Perimenopause: women "can lose up to one-fifth of their bone density during their perimenopausal years"; do not focus on weight; add strength, HIIT/SIT and plyometrics; sleep loss raises cortisol. — Kateryna Moisyeyeva, [Coaching Female Athletes with Perimenopause and Menopause](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coach-female-athletes-perimenopause-menopause/)
- Nutrition-driven HRV drops: inflammatory (processed, high-glycaemic) eating caused "significant daily drops in HRV" in Dr Andrew Flatt with no training change; dehydration lowers HRV and raises RMR. — [The Whole Picture: Nutrition](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/total-load-nutrition/)

### Inferences
- A log-based LEA screen the assistant can run: (a) RHR ≥5 bpm over 30-day baseline for >2 weeks, (b) HRV trending below baseline despite rest days, (c) repeated failure to hit previously attainable interval targets at normal RPE or higher HR for the same power/pace, (d) two or more injuries or a bone stress injury within a season, (e) body-mass decline coinciding with rising training volume, (f) missed/irregular menstrual cycles in the Garmin Women's Health field, (g) illness frequency. Two or more together should trigger a fueling review and a blood panel (ferritin, CBC, vitamin D, B12).
- Under-fueled individual sessions look like high-intensity work done fasted/glycogen-low: elevated HR and RPE for the prescribed power with a fade; the fix is pre-session carbohydrate, not more rest.
- The category's first-line correction is +300–600 kcal/day concentrated around sessions, with specialist referral if amenorrhea or eating-disorder features are present.

### Gaps
- No article quantifies energy availability itself (kcal/kg fat-free mass/day thresholds) or gives a method to compute it from TrainingPeaks kJ data.
- No article gives specific HR-drift or pace-fade percentages that discriminate under-fueling from heat or fitness; the ≤5 % decoupling benchmark is presented generically.
- Male-specific RED-S signs (e.g., testosterone, libido, morning erections) are not covered; the one 16:8 time-restricted-eating study notes lower testosterone/IGF-1 but is not framed as a screening tool.
- No data-based prevalence for male athletes; the female triad article explicitly argues male incidence is "so small as to remain unknown," which conflicts with the IOC statement quoted in the lifestyle-factors article (low BMD risk in male runners/cyclists).

## Key Question 5 — How environment should modify the interpretation of a session (summary rules for a coaching assistant)

### Takeaway
Across the category, the recurring analysis pattern is: read HR relative to power/pace (Pa:HR or Pw:HR), allow up to 5 % decoupling as normal, attribute larger drift to heat, dehydration, or under-fueling in that order of likelihood given the weather and session length, and never grade hot-day or windy-day sessions on output alone.

### Cited Findings
- Analyze 360 is explicitly built to explain "why power, pace, and heart rate drift even when a workout looks the same on paper" by layering core/skin temperature, HSI, breathing and sweat data over the file, with weather context "temperature, humidity, wind, and whether the session was indoor or outdoor." Breathing-rate vs minute-ventilation decoupling reveals fatigue "that heart rate, power, and pace alone often miss"; breathing rate tracks perceived exertion, ventilation tracks internal workload. — [Analyze 360: See and Analyze Every Metric in Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)
- Ultra coaching practice: prescribe by effort and terrain, then evaluate with Normalized Graded Pace, Intensity Factor and Pa:HR; improved durability "shows up as the ability to complete more work at the same perceived effort with less physiological drift." — [Fatigue Resistance in Ultras](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/fatigue-resistance-ultras-molly-seidel-training/)
- Durability definition and test: performance deterioration after ~1,000–2,000 kJ; "a rider's durability in longer endurance events (~90 min+) is a key performance indicator." — [Predicting and Measuring Kilojoules to Determine Athlete Durability](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/predicting-measuring-kilojoules-trainingpeaks/)
- Dehydration-driven HR drift appears after "more than a couple of hours in a warm environment." — [The Whole Picture: Nutrition](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/total-load-nutrition/)
- Heat sessions intentionally produce Z2–Z4 HR at Z1 power (supplementary, not Science tag). — [Heat Training for Cyclists](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/heat-training-for-cyclists/)
- Caffeine lowers RPE for a given output (Japanese cycling study, 60-min session), so caffeinated sessions may show lower RPE at the same HR/power. — [How Endurance Athletes Can Use Caffeine More Effectively](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-endurance-athletes-can-use-caffeine-more-effectively/)
- Sleep restriction raises RPE and slows time-trial performance; "extending sleep duration by 30 percent for three nights or more" removed the deficit. — Phil White, [Using Nutrition for Strategic Sleep in Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/strategic-sleep-nutrition/)

### Inferences
- Interpretation order for an anomalous session: (1) check weather (temperature, humidity, wind, indoor/outdoor); (2) check duration (>2 h raises dehydration probability); (3) check decoupling against the 5 % benchmark and the athlete's own heat-adapted baseline; (4) check reported intake against the duration-tiered carbohydrate/fluid floors in KQ1; (5) check recent sleep, alcohol, caffeine; (6) only then question fitness.
- A hot-weather session with Z1–Z2 power and Z3 HR and ≤20 % power reduction is consistent with a deliberate heat-training session, and should be logged as heat load, not as a failed workout.

### Gaps
- No article gives a decision tree or numeric attribution between heat, dehydration and under-fueling for a given amount of drift; the ordering above is an inference from the sources, not a published rule.
- TrainingPeaks' own Heat Training Load and Heat Adaptation Score metrics are referenced but defined only in external CORE documentation, not in the category.
