## Key Question 1: What exact formulas and constants does TP use (TSS, NP, IF, rTSS/NGP, sTSS, hrTSS, CTL 42 d, ATL 7 d, TSB, VI, EF, decoupling)?

### Takeaway
TSS = (sec × NP × IF)/(FTP × 3600) × 100, with 1 h at FTP = 100 by definition; NP is a 30-s rolling average raised to the 4th power, averaged, then 4th-rooted; IF = NP/FTP (or NGP/threshold pace); CTL and ATL are exponentially weighted averages of daily TSS with default time constants 42 and 7 days (CTL_today = CTL_yesterday + (TSS_today − CTL_yesterday)/42); TSB = yesterday's CTL − yesterday's ATL; VI = NP/AP; EF = NP (or NGP) / average HR; decoupling = (EF_1st half − EF_2nd half)/EF_1st half as a percentage.

### Cited Findings
**TSS**
- TSS formula: "TSS = (sec x NP® x IF®)/(FTP x 3600) x 100" where sec = duration in seconds, NP = Normalized Power, IF = Intensity Factor, FTP = Functional Threshold Power, 3600 = seconds per hour. — [TP Learn, Joe Friel, "Estimating Training Stress Score (TSS)"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/estimating-training-stress-score-tss/); same formula repeated in [TP Learn, Taylor Thomas, "Training with TSS vs. hrTSS"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/training-with-tss-vs-hrtss-whats-the-difference/)
- "By definition, one hour spent at Functional Threshold Power (FTP) is equal to 100 points"; TSS "is conceptually modeled after the heart rate-based training impulse (TRIMP)". Power-based TSS applies to "Bike or Rowing with Power Meter only" and is "the most accurate method". — [TP Help Center, "Training Stress Scores (TSS) Explained"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071944-Training-Stress-Scores-TSS-Explained)
- "A 1-hour cycling activity at maximum steady-state intensity is 100 TSS (111 for running)." — [TP Help Center, "TSS (Training Stress Score)"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071744-TSS-Training-Stress-Score)
- "You can earn more than 100 TSS within a single workout (as long as it is longer than an hour), but never more than 100 TSS per hour"; "100 points earned by a pro is relatively the same as 100 points earned for a beginner because TSS is relative to each person's individual threshold." — [TP Learn, "What Is TSS?"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/what-is-tss/)
- Friel's caveat: "Since FTP is by definition 100 TSS for one hour, a 100 TSS workout cannot last longer than one hour." — [TP Learn, Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/estimating-training-stress-score-tss/)
- Coggan: TSS "takes into account both the intensity (i.e., IF) and the duration of each training session, and might be best viewed as a predictor of the amount of glycogen utilized in each workout." — [TP Learn, Andrew Coggan, "Normalized Power, Intensity Factor and Training Stress Score"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/normalized-power-intensity-factor-training-stress/)
- Coach-blog framing: "The root of all data in TrainingPeaks comes down to duration and intensity which produce a Training Stress Score"; TSS "scores a workout based on duration and intensity as a factor of threshold (Pace, Heart Rate, and Power)." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Andrew Simmons, "A Coach's Guide to ATL, CTL & TSB"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)

**Normalized Power (NP)**
- Step-by-step algorithm: "Step 1: Calculate the rolling average power with a window size of 30 seconds... Step 2: Take each value from step one and take this value to the fourth power... Step 3: Calculate the average of values from the previous step. Step 4: Take the fourth root of the average from the previous step — this value gives us the normalized power." Worked example: 70 s at 400 W, 30 s surge at 600 W, 20 s at 400 W gives average power 450 W and NP 478 W "as calculated by TrainingPeaks". — [Science, Tom Epton, "Understanding Normalized Power Calculations to Coach Cyclists"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/normalized-power-how-coaches-use/)
- Rationale (Coggan): the algorithm incorporates that "physiological responses to rapid changes in exercise intensity are not instantaneous" and that responses such as "glycogen utilization, lactate production, stress hormone levels" are "curvilinearly, rather than linearly, related to exercise intensity"; NP is "an estimate of the power that you could have maintained for the same physiological 'cost' if your power output had been perfectly constant"; NP applies to rides or segments "longer than 30 seconds". — [TP Learn, Coggan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/normalized-power-intensity-factor-training-stress/)
- Epton on the 30 s window and exponent 4: both are "a population level average"; the window and exponent "are selected because they reflect that functionally, physiological responses are not instantaneous and don't have a linear relationship with intensity". — [Science, Epton](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/normalized-power-how-coaches-use/)

**Intensity Factor (IF)**
- "IF is simply the ratio of the normalized power as described above to your threshold power." Example: NP 210 W with FTP 280 W gives IF 0.75; same ride with FTP 300 W gives 0.70. — [TP Learn, Coggan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/normalized-power-intensity-factor-training-stress/)
- For running, "Intensity Factor (IF) for rTSS: ... the ratio of the Normalized Graded Pace to the runner's functional threshold running pace". — [TP Help Center, TSS Explained](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071944-Training-Stress-Scores-TSS-Explained)
- Glossary definition: IF is "The relative intensity of a given workout without regard for the duration. One hour at maximum effort is 1.0". — [TP Learn, "Glossary of TrainingPeaks Metrics"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)

**rTSS and NGP**
- rTSS "is based on pace ... the elements of the normalized graded pace (NGP), relative to the functional threshold pace. Coupled with the duration of the workout"; requirements: "Workout time series with pace and elevation data, user threshold pace for run/walk"; "rTSS is the second most accurate method". NGP is "The adjusted pace ... that reflects the changes in grade and intensity that contribute to the physiological cost of running on varied terrain." — [TP Help Center, TSS Explained](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071944-Training-Stress-Scores-TSS-Explained)
- McGregor (creator of NGP/rTSS): threshold pace is "the fastest running pace you could sustain for one hour"; the calculation "makes adjustments for hills (for example, a hilly 40-minute run at a steady pace of 8:00/mile would generate a higher TSS than a flat version of the same run) and even for the fact that running at any given pace becomes more physiologically stressful the longer that pace is sustained." — [TP Learn, Stephen McGregor, "Running Training Stress Score (rTSS) Explained"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/running-training-stress-score-rtss-explained/)

**sTSS (swim)**
- Platform definition: "Swim Training Stress Score (sTSS) is based on your functional threshold swim pace, total distance covered, and total moving duration (duration not including rest periods)"; requirements: "Workout moving duration, distance, user threshold speed for swimming". — [TP Help Center, TSS Explained](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071944-Training-Stress-Scores-TSS-Explained)
- Manual method (Fitzgerald and McGregor): because "water presents more resistance than air", the swim IF "should be cubed as opposed to squared". Procedure: total distance / moving time in m/min = normalized swim speed (NSS); IF = NSS / swim FTP; "Swim TSS = (Intensity Factor cubed) x hours x 100". Worked example: FTP 75 m/min, 1,600 m in 23:10 (0.386 h) gives 69 m/min, IF 0.92, TSS = 0.778 × 0.386 × 100 = 30.1. Swim FTP from a 30-min test: "multiply by two and subtract 2.5 percent"; or critical speed = (D_long − D_short)/(T_long − T_short). — [TP Learn, "Calculating Swimming TSS Score"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/calculating-swimming-tss-score/)

**hrTSS and tTSS**
- "Heart Rate Training Stress Score (hrTSS) is based on time in heart rate zones (based on users' lactate threshold heart rate)"; requirements: time series, HR data, threshold HR. "hrTSS will only be used if there is not enough data to calculate TSS, rTSS, or sTSS". tTSS (TRIMPS TSS) "is based on average heart rate, the total duration of the workout, and the person's physiological heart rate properties (resting heart rate, threshold heart rate, and maximum heart rate)" and is used only when TSS, rTSS, sTSS and hrTSS are all unavailable. A "TSS*" asterisk marks manually entered TSS. — [TP Help Center, TSS Explained](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071944-Training-Stress-Scores-TSS-Explained)
- hrTSS "calculation is made using an estimate of the amount of accumulated TSS in an hour, given the level of exertion." — [TP Learn, Thomas](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/training-with-tss-vs-hrtss-whats-the-difference/)
- Friel's estimation examples (RPE/HR-zone table): "A 30-minute workout at an average RPE of 6 would be a TSS estimate of 35 (70 x 0.5)"; "A steady 90-minute workout at an average heart rate of high 2 zone would be a TSS estimate of 90 (60 x 1.5)"; 15 min high zone 1 (TSS 10) + 30 min zone 5a (TSS 50) + 15 min low zone 1 (TSS 5) = 65. — [TP Learn, Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/estimating-training-stress-score-tss/)
- Manual estimate guide for trail runs without HR: "Easy = 30 TSS/hour, Moderate = 60 TSS/hour, Hard = 90 TSS/hour". — [TP Help Center, "Low rTSS and Trail Running"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/205229730-Low-rTSS-and-Trail-Running)

**CTL / ATL / TSB (PMC)**
- CTL: "TrainingPeaks calculates CTL, by default, as the exponentially weighted average of daily TSS for the past 42 days (6 weeks)... in effect, CTL represents the training an athlete has done in the past 3 months". Formula: "CTLtoday = CTLyesterday + (TSStoday - CTLyesterday)(1/CTL time constant)". Planned-workout TSS is included in projected CTL until the workout is completed. — [TP Help Center, "Fitness (CTL)"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071884-Fitness-CTL-)
- ATL: "exponentially weighted average of daily TSS for the past 7 days... in effect, ATL represents the training an athlete has done in the past two weeks". Formula: "ATLtoday = ATLyesterday + (TSStoday - ATLyesterday)(1/ATL time constant)". — [TP Help Center, "Fatigue (ATL)"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071894-Fatigue-ATL-)
- TSB: "Form (TSB) = Yesterday's Fitness (CTL) - Yesterday's Fatigue (ATL)"; "The TSB value for Today is calculated from the previous day's training"; positive = "Fresh, or over-adapted", neutral = adapted, negative = "not adapted to a training load"; "TSB is not as a predictor of performance but as a measure of how adapted an athlete is to their training load." — [TP Help Center, "Form (TSB)"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071764-Form-TSB)
- Same constants in coach-blog language: CTL is "an ongoing average of your daily training (TSS load) specifically looking at the last six weeks or 42 days"; ATL "calculates a rolling average of your workout TSS over the last seven days"; "It takes 42 days to get concrete CTL when starting fresh." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- Science-category statement: CTL "is defined as the forty-two day rolling average of the daily Training Stress Score (TSS) of that athlete's workouts." — [Science, Kyle Wolfe, "Maximizing Athlete Fitness on Limited Time"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/maximizing-athlete-fitness-on-limited-time/)
- "If your Fitness Constant and Fatigue Constant are the same, it will always show a TSB of 0." — [TP Help Center, "What is wrong with my Performance Management Chart?"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/115004969547-What-is-wrong-with-my-Performance-Management-Chart)
- Ramp rate defined as "The change in CTL over time, usually the last seven days." — [TP Learn, Glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)

**VI, EF, decoupling**
- VI: "Variability Index is calculated by dividing your Normalized Power ... with your average power"; example: AP 239 W, NP 302 W gives VI 1.26. — [TP athlete blog, Dave Schell, "An Introduction to TrainingPeaks Metrics: Part Two"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/an-introduction-to-more-trainingpeaks-metrics-part-two/)
- EF: "EF represents the Normalized Power (NP) of the athlete divided by their heart rate." — [Science, Alan Couzens, "3 Key Metrics of Effective Athlete Data Analysis for Coaches"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/3-key-metrics-of-effective-data-analysis-for-coaches/)
- EF for running: "TrainingPeaks converts your Normalized Graded Pace (NGP) into Normalized Graded Speed (NGS) and divides it by your average heart rate". — [TP athlete blog, Joe Friel, "Efficiency Factor and Decoupling"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/efficiency-factor-and-decoupling/)
- Decoupling: "TrainingPeaks compares the Efficiency Factors for the two halves of the workout or for a selected workout segment... The difference between the EF for the first half and the EF for the second is divided by EF for the first half. This produces a percentage." — [TP athlete blog, Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/efficiency-factor-and-decoupling/); platform: "The platform calculates this by comparing the first half of a steady effort to the second half", displayed as Pa:Hr or Pw:Hr. — [Coach blog (Performance), Joe Friel, "Aerobic Decoupling and Heart Rate Drift Explained"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)
- Platform requirement: for runs "you must choose rTSS (which is based on pace) for this advanced metric [EF] to be displayed." — [TP Help Center, "Aerobic Decoupling (Pw:Hr and Pa:HR) and Efficiency Factor (EF)"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071724-Aerobic-Decoupling-Pw-Hr-and-Pa-HR-and-Efficiency-Factor-EF)

### Inferences
- The Science-category articles assume the formulas rather than state them; a coaching assistant should cite the Help Center (constants) and Coggan/Friel Learn articles (formulas) as the canonical source, and the coach-blog articles for interpretation.
- The Help Center's "111 for running" figure for one hour at threshold conflicts with the "never more than 100 TSS per hour" rule stated elsewhere; it likely reflects the rTSS algorithm's duration weighting described by McGregor, but no source explains the number.

### Gaps
- The hrTSS zone-to-TSS/hour table is an image on TP pages and could not be extracted; only Friel's worked examples (zone 1 high ≈ 40/h implied by 15 min = 10; zone 2 high = 60/h; zone 5a = 100/h implied by 30 min = 50; RPE 6 = 70/h) are available in text.
- The exact NGP algorithm is not published in any fetched page (only its purpose).
- The platform's sTSS implementation is described only qualitatively; whether it still uses the cubed-IF approach is not stated on the Help page.
- "Adjusting CTL/ATL Parameters" and "TSS calculation troubleshooting" help pages could not be read (JavaScript wall).

## Key Question 2: What CTL ramp rate and TSB ranges do the articles recommend, and for which athlete types (plus IF-by-race and TSS-per-workout scales)?

### Takeaway
The coach-blog guidance is consistent: ramp CTL at most 5–7 points per week (even for very fit athletes), avoid TSB below −30, train productively in the −10 to −30 band, race at roughly +15 to +25 (Friel) or "slightly negative up to +25" (TP staff), and expect most amateurs to plateau near CTL ~110; IF by event ranges from 0.60–0.70 (age-group Ironman) to 1.05–1.15 (short TT).

### Cited Findings
**Ramp rate**
- "Very fit athletes can increase their CTL up to five-to-seven points a week." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- When building an ATP, "create hypothetical CTL targets, making sure we stay within the five to seven CTL points/week guidelines"; example athlete (35 y, 6 years training, recovery every 4 weeks) targets CTL 72 for one marathon and 80 for the next, previous best marathon at CTL 68 "with a great taper". — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Andrew Simmons, "Basic ATP Planning with Ramp Rate"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/basic-atp-planning-with-ramp-rate/)
- "It's best to stick to what you know: generally avoid a TSB of greater than -30, keep ramp rate to five to seven points of CTL, and minimize overloading in any direction more than 10%." — [Science, Andrew Simmons, "How To Design An Endurance Athlete's Total Training Load"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/designing-an-athletes-total-load/)
- Glossary: "A ramp rate that is too high may lead to overtraining or injury; too low may not gain fitness quickly or effectively." — [TP Learn, Glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
- Simmons: "Steady progression is key to preventing injuries. Large spikes in CTL correlate with a large amount of incurred training stress... ensure that a large amount of training load pairs with a substantial recovery". — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- The 2025 Athlete Home update presents "Training Readiness and Ramp Rates" drawn from WKO "into ranges" for athletes (no numeric ranges given in the article). — [Coach blog, Tony Viton, "Athlete Home Update: Demystifying the PMC"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/athlete-home-feature-pmc/)

**TSB windows**
- "Joe Friel has mentioned numerous times that an ideal TSB range for a peak performance falls between +15 and +25. While zero indicates the athlete has reached a training equilibrium, complete recovery is often needed for peak performance, especially for long-distance racing." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- "The ideal training zone for TSB falls between -10 and -30. If athletes push beyond -30 they are headed for extreme strain or have recently completed an extensive race effort. Numbers extending beyond -30 will require consecutive days of rest"; "if you start to see +15 to +25 TSB you are extensively recovered and essentially losing fitness." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- TP staff: "There is no single Form that works for every athlete, but as a general rule of thumb, you would want to be slightly negative up to positive 25. If your Form becomes too high, it may indicate that you tapered too much and have lost fitness." Also: "by tapering, you can shed fatigue at a greater rate than you lose fitness". — [TP Learn, "What is the Performance Management Chart?"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/what-is-the-performance-management-chart/)
- Glossary: TSB "High positive values may indicate undertraining; high negative values may indicate overtraining." — [TP Learn, Glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
- Overreaching detection: record wellness/fatigue metrics "especially during periods of significantly increased loads when metrics like TSB and the acute chronic training load ratio get worse." — [Science, "The Fine Line Between Functional Training and Overreaching"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fine-line-between-functional-training-and-overreaching/)
- TSB framed as an example of an Acute:Chronic training load ratio, which research shows predicts injury better than traditional metrics. — [Science, Simon Wegerif, "The Whole Picture: An Introduction to Total Load"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-an-introduction-to-total-load/)

**CTL benchmarks and per-workout TSS scales**
- CTL ceiling: "the metric CTL ... can help you track how close you are to your peak training load or the point at which further training won't lead to improvements. For most amateur athletes, this point starts around 110 CTL." — [Science, Jakub Novak, "The 4 Signs of Overtraining"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- Workout TSS relative to CTL (example CTL 50): "Hard Workout ... TSS score that is 50%-100% above your current CTL (75 – 100 TSS)"; "Moderate Workout ... 25% above your current CTL (60-70 TSS)"; "Easy Workout ... 10-25% below your current CTL (35-40 TSS)". — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- Coggan's single-session recovery scale: "TSS less than 150 – low (recovery generally complete by following day); 150-300 – medium (some residual fatigue may be present the next day, but gone by 2nd day); 300-450 – high (some residual fatigue may be present even after 2 days); Greater than 450 – very high (residual fatigue lasting several days likely)". — [TP Learn, Coggan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/normalized-power-intensity-factor-training-stress/)
- Time-crunched cyclist example: a 40 km TT ≈ 100 TSS, "a one hour moderate ride would be about 50 TSS"; five rides of ~67–101 TSS (6–8 h/week) "would give you a potential CTL of almost 60 points"; the athlete won five regional races averaging 8.25 h/week. — [Science, Wolfe](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/maximizing-athlete-fitness-on-limited-time/)
- Age-group triathlon benchmarks (95 athletes, 34,731 sessions): long-course athletes averaged "615 minutes of training per week, 171 km per week, and 574 TSS per week"; short-course "507 minutes, 118 km, and 452 TSS per week"; vs General phase, load was "approximately 21% higher in the Specific phase, 14% lower in the Taper/Race/Post-race phase, and 38% lower in the Off-season"; sex and age group were not significantly associated with weekly TSS. — [Coach blog (Triathlon tag), Leighton Wells, "Age-Group Triathlon Training Load"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/age-group-triathlon-training-load/)
- Estimated starting CTL: a cyclist training ~10 h/week for two months "should be approximately 70"; seeded values must be interpreted "cautiously until sufficient data are available (about 4-6 weeks)" and then reset to zero. — [TP Help Center, "Estimate Starting Fitness (CTL)"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/230903988-Estimate-Starting-Fitness-CTL)
- Race-prediction heuristic (working theory, ~25 athletes, 20/25 marathon predictions consistent): a proper taper is when race-day TSS "doesn't push their response or predicted CTL beyond the highest CTL they achieved in training"; e.g. an athlete peaking at CTL 63 "should be able to perform an effort that produces enough TSS load to produce a rise of 63". — [Coach blog, Andrew Simmons, "Athletes' Running Data"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/athletes-running-data/)

**IF by session and race type**
- Coggan's session table: "<0.75 recovery rides; 0.75-0.85 endurance-paced training rides; 0.85-0.95 tempo rides, aerobic and anaerobic interval workouts (work and rest periods combined), longer (>2.5 h) road races; 0.95-1.05 lactate threshold intervals (work period only), shorter (<2.5 h) road races, criteriums, circuit races, longer (e.g., 40 km) TTs; 1.05-1.15 shorter (e.g., 15 km) TTs, track points race; >1.15 prologue TT, track pursuit, track miss-and-out." — [TP Learn, Coggan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/normalized-power-intensity-factor-training-stress/)
- Friel's race table (from The Power Meter Handbook): "Ironman (age group) 0.60-0.70; Ironman (elite) / Half-Ironman (age group) 0.70-0.79; Half-Ironman (elite) / Long road race / Mountain bike marathon 0.80-0.89; Olympic and sprint triathlons / Long time trial / Short mountain bike race / Criterium 0.90-1.04; Short time trial 1.05-1.15". General endurance work "falls in the 60-70% range, while a harder tempo workout would be closer to 80-90%". — [TP Learn, "An Introduction to TrainingPeaks Metrics"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/an-introduction-to-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
- Couzens: long-distance specialists need "the ability to hold high percentages of their aerobic maximum for long periods of time, i.e. to have a relatively high IF"; IF-versus-duration on the Mean Maximal Power curve distinguishes Ironman from sprint phenotypes. — [Science, Couzens](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/3-key-metrics-of-effective-data-analysis-for-coaches/)
- VI targets: for a TT or triathlon bike leg "we would expect these numbers to be pretty close, resulting in a VI of 1.05 or less"; criteriums and MTB "may result in a VI of 1.3 or higher". — [TP athlete blog, Schell](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/an-introduction-to-more-trainingpeaks-metrics-part-two/); Couzens: "There is a unique, optimal power distribution for every athlete and every course. The VI number helps us to quantify that". — [Science, Couzens](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/3-key-metrics-of-effective-data-analysis-for-coaches/)

**Detraining / time off (context for interpreting CTL drops)**
- Reviews cited: "it takes 15 days of inactivity to create a 4% decrease in VO2max" and impairments appear "following 35–42 days of light-moderate exercise" in highly trained athletes; short vacations do not threaten fitness. — [Science, Phil White, "Vacations Are Valuable for Athlete Fitness"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/vacations-valuable-athlete-fitness/)

### Inferences
- The 5–7 CTL/week ceiling is presented as a universal upper bound (Simmons applies it to "very fit athletes" and to a busy 35-year-old amateur alike); no article gives a lower ramp figure for novices or masters, so the assistant should treat 5–7 as a maximum, not a target.
- The two TSB race windows (+15 to +25 vs "slightly negative to +25") overlap; the conservative synthesis is: race between roughly +5 and +25, aim higher (+15 to +25) for long-course events, and never above +25.
- Wells' phase percentages (+21% specific, −14% taper, −38% off-season) are the only large-sample benchmark in the category and are explicitly "broad reference points, not rigid rules".

### Gaps
- No article in the Science category gives athlete-type-specific ramp rates (e.g., masters vs U23) or sport-specific TSB windows; the numbers come from two Coaching-101 articles and one Science article by the same author (Simmons).
- The "Suggested Weekly TSS and Target CTL" help tables are images and could not be read.
- WKO "Training Readiness" ranges surfaced in Athlete Home are not published numerically in any fetched article.

## Key Question 3: What do they say about EF and decoupling thresholds as fitness indicators?

### Takeaway
Decoupling below 5% over the event-relevant duration is the consistent benchmark for sound aerobic endurance (with 5–10% moderate and >10% too intense or under-trained per Friel), EF should rise across weeks of similar steady aerobic sessions, and both metrics are only valid for steady (low-VI), sub-threshold efforts of 20 min or more under controlled conditions.

### Cited Findings
- Friel's three-band rule: "< 5% decoupling: Strong aerobic endurance at that intensity; 5–10% decoupling: Moderate endurance limitations or fatigue; > 10% decoupling: The effort was likely above aerobic threshold or the athlete lacks the endurance to sustain it". — [Coach blog (Performance), Friel, "Aerobic Decoupling and Heart Rate Drift Explained"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)
- Duration benchmarks for "staying coupled": cycling "two to four hours at aerobic threshold (AeT)" (2 h if race <2 h, e.g. crit or sprint/Olympic bike leg; match race duration for 2–4 h events, e.g. 2.5 h for a half-distance bike leg; 4 h cap for Ironman/long road races); running "one to two hours at AeT" (1 h if race <1 h; match duration for 1–2 h races; 2 h cap beyond). AeT sessions "once or twice per week during the Base phase"; "When an athlete can hold the target duration with less than 5% decoupling, it's a strong sign that aerobic endurance is well developed" and the athlete is ready for the Build phase. — [Coach blog (Performance), Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)
- Causes of drift: "rising core temperature, dehydration, glycogen depletion, and cardiovascular fatigue"; high decoupling may mean intensity too high, insufficient base, environmental stress, or accumulated fatigue; "Over time, you should see heart rate drift decrease at the same intensity as aerobic fitness improves. That trend is often a clearer indicator of aerobic development than single test results." — [Coach blog (Performance), Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)
- Friel (original 2011 piece): "I like to see athletes achieve a decoupling of 5% or less (negative numbers are ... assumed to be good results)"; "if an athlete's decoupling is consistently 5% or less for steady-state aerobic workouts then his or her aerobic endurance is sound"; validity requires the effort to be "fully aerobic (below the lactate/anaerobic threshold) and steady (low Variability Index)". — [TP athlete blog, Friel, "Efficiency Factor and Decoupling"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/efficiency-factor-and-decoupling/)
- Platform rule: "A smaller change in EF (less than 5%) from the first half to the second half may indicate improving aerobic endurance"; "Perform a sustained, steady effort greater 20 minutes or greater"; "Aerobic Decoupling values for efforts under 20 minutes in duration aren't as valid"; "EF is only a valid metric for aerobic, steady-state workouts. Shorter intervals at higher intensities require anaerobic energy contribution which invalidates EF as an aerobic metric." — [TP Help Center, EF and Decoupling](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071724-Aerobic-Decoupling-Pw-Hr-and-Pa-HR-and-Efficiency-Factor-EF)
- Glossary gives a wider tolerance: "A lower value (<5-8%) for long, steady rides may indicate improved aerobic endurance." — [TP Learn, Glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
- Science-category applications: "For longer events, most consider 5% the maximum amount of decoupling for optimal performance"; in a 6.5 h ride, decoupling was "about 8%" once Heat Strain Index exceeded 3.0, showing heat as a confounder; HSI zone 2 (1.0–2.9) is where "Heart rate is starting to decouple from power/pace". — [Science, "See and Analyze Every Metric"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)
- Run coaching use: "Within the 0-5% range, we're in a happy place"; an interval with 8.87% decoupling during the first 4 min (pace reached too quickly) versus 1.47% for the remaining 8 min shows the need to inspect how fast the HR plateau is reached. — [Coach blog, Simmons, "Athletes' Running Data"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/athletes-running-data/)
- EF as dose-response: "When EF is tracked concurrently with the athlete's Chronic Training Load (CTL), we have the beginnings of a simple but powerful model of training 'dose' versus training 'response'"; EF "assumes that more power generated on fewer heartbeats equals more fitness. This is a generally safe assumption, at least for a given athlete." — [Science, Couzens](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/3-key-metrics-of-effective-data-analysis-for-coaches/)
- EF protocol: compare "similar workouts over several weeks"; control "level of pre-workout fatigue, equipment, course, weather conditions, altitude, pre-workout nutrition (especially stimulants such as caffeine), warm-up and perhaps even time of day"; "If you are making good aerobic progress, then your EF will rise over the course of a few weeks." — [TP athlete blog, Friel](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/efficiency-factor-and-decoupling/)
- Schell: EF "is not useful on its own or for comparing yourself to other athletes"; "Schedule a steadily paced aerobic benchmark run or ride every one to two weeks and track your Efficiency Factor over time. Once your EF plateaus, it may be time to move on to the next phase"; if decoupling >5% "look at environmental factors such as the temperature or your hydration status. If those things seem normal, you may need some additional aerobic conditioning." — [TP athlete blog, Schell](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/an-introduction-to-more-trainingpeaks-metrics-part-two/)
- Running-power analogue: efficiency tracked as power-to-speed ratio; "An athlete becomes more efficient if the power needed to run at a given speed decreases"; a power-to-speed ratio that does not deteriorate under fatigue indicates preserved running economy. — [Science, Tom Epton, "Coaching Running Power Better"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-running-power-better/)
- Triathletes who struggle running off the bike typically show "a reduced efficiency factor, but their power values remain largely unaffected", so pacing by run power is recommended. — [Science, Philip Hatzis, "Is Running with Power Worth It?"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/is-running-with-power-worth-it/)

### Inferences
- The operational rule set is: (a) only evaluate EF/decoupling on steady, sub-AeT/sub-LT efforts of ≥20 min (ideally the event-relevant 1–4 h); (b) <5% = pass, 5–10% = investigate heat/hydration/fatigue before concluding under-training, >10% = intensity too high or base lacking; (c) trend EF upward across repeated benchmark sessions and treat a plateau as a cue to change phase.
- The Glossary's "<5-8%" is looser than every other TP source; treat 5% as the default and 8% as the outer tolerance for long rides.

### Gaps
- The Glossary entry states that "A drop in this number [EF] over time for similar workouts indicates a higher power or pace for a given level of effort and an increase in fitness", which contradicts Friel, Couzens, Schell and the Help Center (all say EF should rise). This appears to be an error in the Glossary; the report should flag it rather than adopt it. — [TP Learn, Glossary](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/glossary-of-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
- No article gives numeric EF targets (absolute values are athlete-specific by design).

## Key Question 4: What pitfalls do they flag (stale thresholds, mixing HR/power TSS, indoor vs outdoor, NP misuse, swim TSS, bad PMC data)?

### Takeaway
The dominant warning is that every metric is only as good as the threshold behind it: wrong or stale FTP/pace/LTHR corrupts TSS, IF, CTL and the whole ATP; secondary warnings cover hrTSS under-counting intense/variable work, NP being invalid under ~10 min and a poor judge of performance, run power or rough trails distorting rTSS, swim TSS ignoring rest and technique, strength work not counting toward TSS, and single bad TSS values wrecking the PMC.

### Cited Findings
**Thresholds and zones**
- "Incorrect zones will undercut any planning and projecting you're doing on behalf of the athlete. Athletes will need regular workouts and fitness tests to help them manage their progression"; "It's imperative to set the athlete's zones correctly before you can have any confidence in the information being produced." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- "Programming with the workout builder demands that zones as well as the workout itself are set correctly." — [Science, Simmons, Total Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/designing-an-athletes-total-load/)
- "Most of the metrics in TrainingPeaks are based on a Functional Threshold (Power, Pace, or Heart Rate), so it is important that you have this right before jumping ahead to some of the other metrics." — [TP Learn, Intro to Metrics](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/an-introduction-to-trainingpeaks-metrics/)
- IF as a threshold-staleness detector: "an IF of more than 1.05 for a race that is approximately 1 hour in duration is often a sign that the rider's threshold power is actually greater than that presently entered into the program"; NP from a hard ~1 h race "can therefore often be used to provide an initial estimate of a rider's threshold power". — [TP Learn, Coggan](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/normalized-power-intensity-factor-training-stress/)
- Lab vs field power: "If an athlete tests on a lab ergometer that reads 5% higher than their power meter, then builds zones off those lab numbers, the training prescription will be off from day one." — [Science, "Lactate Threshold Testing for Better Performance"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lactate-threshold-testing-better-performance/)
- Retroactive recalculation caveat: "changing the source of the TSS calculation will use your current threshold settings, so if you change the TSS selection on an older workout and your threshold has since changed, you may get a result that isn't accurate for that point in time." — [TP Help Center, "Can I change TSS to Heart Rate, Power, or Pace?"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/115007309027-Can-I-change-TSS-to-Heart-Rate-Power-or-Pace)
- Set sport-specific thresholds: "it is best practice to set sport-specific zones using the Add Activity dropdown. If you have a different threshold for each sport type (ie, heart rate for running and cycling), you will want to set thresholds and zones for each sport"; default values are used for any sport without its own setting. — [TP Help Center, "How to Set your Thresholds and Calculate your Zones"](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204073584)
- Do not judge fitness by threshold alone: "Assessing your fitness by only using Threshold Pace, FTP, or 100m pace average is a dangerous premise." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), "The Fitness Testing We Should Be Doing"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fitness-testing-we-should-be-doing/)

**HR-based TSS (hrTSS) and mixing sources**
- hrTSS "is quite accurate with steady-state effort workouts, but is less accurate for workouts with highly fluctuating efforts"; the platform's priority order is TSS (power) > rTSS > sTSS > hrTSS > tTSS. — [TP Help Center, TSS Explained](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/204071944-Training-Stress-Scores-TSS-Explained)
- "This metric begins to fall away when shorter and more intense efforts occur. The heart doesn't respond rapidly enough to weight efforts above threshold properly. This makes the 'cost' of the workout seem much lower than it really is"; switching to power makes workouts "look" more stressful; hrTSS is best for "long tempo and sub-threshold work"; TSS accuracy "influences core metrics such as Fitness, Form, Fatigue and ramp rate." — [TP Learn, Thomas](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/training-with-tss-vs-hrtss-whats-the-difference/)
- Weekly average HR is too blunt: it "was not significantly associated with sex, age, race distance preference, or training phase", so coaches should "look beyond weekly averages and examine session-level intensity distribution". — [Coach blog (Triathlon), Wells](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/age-group-triathlon-training-load/)
- HR-derived metrics (EF, decoupling) "can be impacted by heat, stress, dehydration, or other environmental factors." — [TP athlete blog, Schell](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/an-introduction-to-more-trainingpeaks-metrics-part-two/)

**NP misuse**
- "At TrainingPeaks, coaches are taught to dismiss NP values for durations under 10 minutes... The 30-second window should be less than or equal to 5% of the overall interval. NP should not be used to analyze the success of particular intervals during a session on this basis, nor should it judge the difficulty of short periods within races." — [Science, Epton](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/normalized-power-how-coaches-use/)
- "Average power is more appropriate than NP on long climbs or during time trials. NP should not be used as a measure of judging performance"; "NP may underestimate the load on an athlete new to the sport but overestimate the load on a very well-trained athlete" because the exponent is a population fit. — [Science, Epton](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/normalized-power-how-coaches-use/)

**Running: power, trails, GPS**
- "If you see TSS values for running workouts that are unrealistically high or low for the perceived effort or compared to recent similar workouts, your device may be recording running power, and you will need to adjust your zone settings accordingly. Many new devices have running power automatically turned on"; coaches "must ensure their athlete has accurate and current thresholds through regular testing. This is even more important with running than cycling due to the greater injury risk." — [Science, Epton, Coaching Running Power Better](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-running-power-better/)
- "If TSS is calculated using power and the athlete's form erodes during raceday, there is a good likelihood that the TSS value will end up higher than anticipated". — [Science, Hatzis](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/is-running-with-power-worth-it/)
- Trail running: NGP "cannot fully account for ... the surface, wind, short steep pitches up and down that GPS can't detect well, and the rockiness/rootiness/general technical difficulty of trails", so switch those runs to hrTSS (requires HR data and a correct running LTHR) or estimate 30/60/90 TSS per hour. — [TP Help Center, Low rTSS and Trail Running](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/205229730-Low-rTSS-and-Trail-Running)

**Swim TSS**
- Manual/distance-based swim TSS "neglected" the effects of rest periods, "lacks the exponential weighting of higher intensities", ignores "differences between different swim strokes and the rather substantial differences in efficiency that result from good or poor technique", and neglects "flip turns and push-offs". — [TP Learn, Calculating Swimming TSS Score](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/calculating-swimming-tss-score/)

**Load not captured / PMC data quality**
- "Strength workouts do not factor into athlete TSS data; therefore, coaches should be aware of their athletes' responses to the overall training load" (an estimated TSS can be entered manually). — [Science, Simmons, Total Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/designing-an-athletes-total-load/)
- TSS is a dose, not a response: "TSS was treated as a training-dose metric... It does not directly measure how well an athlete absorbed that work"; interpret alongside "subjective feedback, readiness, sleep, HRV, resting heart rate, mood, soreness, performance trends, and broader life stress." — [Coach blog (Triathlon), Wells](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/age-group-triathlon-training-load/)
- Total load: "Stress = stress, regardless of whether it is served up in training or from a personal source." — [Science, Caitlin Glenn Sapp, "When Does Recovery Become More Important Than Training?"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/when-does-recovery-become-more-important-than-training/); life stress is "often a hidden contributing force in the injury history of athletes." — [Science, Wegerif](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-an-introduction-to-total-load/)
- Bad data: "Bad data or bad settings can throw off the Performance Management Chart"; symptoms are "very high CTL and TSB scores" and most red dots on the x-axis; fix by finding the outlier (e.g. "TSS: 9999.0") and entering a realistic TSS or 0. — [TP Help Center, What is wrong with my PMC?](https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/115004969547-What-is-wrong-with-my-Performance-Management-Chart)
- New athletes: "It takes 42 days to get concrete CTL when starting fresh. Make sure you've had a chance to review all past data and ensure that the PMC is an accurate reflection of the athlete's recent training." — [Coach blog (Coaching-101), Simmons](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-atl-ctl-tsb/)
- Overtraining cross-checks: resting HR "5 bpm above their average indicates they need more recovery time"; HRV "lower than usual, for a longer period of time, despite taking recovery days" signals overreach. — [Science, Novak](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- Taper psychology: athletes "have been watching that blue line, CTL, rise and rise, only to watch it fall in the weeks before the race"; compliance colours in TP are based on duration, distance or TSS versus planned. — [Science, Tim Ballintine, "Rethinking the Taper Phase"](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/rethinking-the-taper-phase/)

### Inferences
- A practical audit checklist derivable from these sources: (1) confirm sport-specific thresholds are current (re-test or use IF >1.05 in ~1 h races as a trigger); (2) check which TSS source each workout used and avoid comparing hrTSS weeks with power weeks; (3) inspect run files for auto-enabled running power; (4) treat swim TSS as approximate; (5) add manual TSS for strength or accept that CTL under-counts it; (6) scan the PMC for outlier TSS before trusting CTL/TSB.
- None of the fetched articles addresses indoor-vs-outdoor differences (trainer FTP vs road FTP) explicitly; the closest statement is the lab-ergometer-vs-power-meter offset warning.

### Gaps
- No Science-category article discusses indoor versus outdoor thresholds or NP differences; the Kevin Poulton indoor-training podcast page contains no metric guidance.
- The Help Center pages "Why do my running TSS values seem wrong?", "TSS calculation troubleshooting" and "Adjusting CTL/ATL Parameters" (which would document when to change the 42/7 constants) could not be retrieved.
- No article quantifies how much a stale FTP inflates TSS (e.g. percent TSS error per percent FTP error); this follows mathematically from TSS ∝ IF² but is not stated in any fetched TP source.
