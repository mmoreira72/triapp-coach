## Key Question 1 — Which test protocols and correction factors do the articles recommend for bike, run and swim (incl. CP/W′, VO2max, MMP, zone models)?

### Takeaway
The category's own position (2019 → 2026) moved from "FTP = 95 % of a 20-min best or a 60-min TT" towards "derive threshold from the whole power-duration curve (mFTP/CP), sanity-check with lactate (LT1/LT2) and the 90-day MMP curve"; for running it still relies on time-trial/race-based pace or HR thresholds, and for swimming on repeat-based T-pace tests (3×300, broken 1k) rather than a single formula.

### Cited Findings

**Bike — FTP field tests and correction factors**
- FTP "is commonly defined as the highest power output an athlete can steadily sustain for 40-80 minutes… In practice, FTP is rarely measured directly through a maximal 60-minute effort. Instead, it's commonly estimated from shorter tests, most notably 95% of the mean maximal power during a 20-minute time trial." (Liam Holohan, 2026) — [FTP vs. Critical Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/ftp-vs-critical-power/) **[outside Science listing; linked from category articles]**
- "The traditional 95% correction from a 20-minute effort assumes athletes have similar fatigue characteristics, which is often not the case. Athletes with a larger anaerobic contribution may overestimate sustainable metabolic steady state, whereas highly aerobic athletes may underestimate it." FTP values "are also sensitive to the testing protocol used" (citing Borszcz 2018). (2026) — [FTP vs. Critical Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/ftp-vs-critical-power/)
- Coach protocols listed for FTP: "Critical Power 60 (CP 60) or 40km Time Trial — Warm Up: 30-45 minute progressive warm-up; Main Set: 60 Minute Time Trial/40km Time Trial; Cooldown 15-20 min" and "Critical Power 20 (.95 x Multiplier) — Warm Up: 20-30 min progressive; Main Set: 20-Minute Best Effort (multiply results by .95 to get a 60-minute equivalent / FTP); Cooldown 15-20 min". "Most critical power tests are shorter than 60 minutes and, as such will require a multiplier to achieve a 60-minute FTP number." (Andrew Simmons, pub. 2020, upd. 2026) — [Athlete Zone Setting and Testing Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/) **[outside Science listing; "Related" link from the lactate article]**
- Alternative to a 20-min test: "Segment Hunting" — 2 attempts at a known Strava segment/climb (sight lap, then full-gas), "Having athletes use Strava segments or known routes that replicate real-world training scenarios often produce clearer results than what an FTP test can tell you." (2020/2026) — [Zone Setting Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/); same protocol at 1:00/3:00/5:00/8:00/10:00+ durations in [The Fitness Testing We Should Be Doing](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fitness-testing-we-should-be-doing/) (Simmons, 2020, upd. 2023)
- Older Science-category definition: "FTP is the highest average power that a rider can maintain during 40 to 60 minutes… FTP is synonym for anaerobic threshold" and "FTP says little about how well you can sprint"; authors propose a "Power Speed Profile" test across short (5 s–1 min), medium (1–10 min, most commonly 4–5 min) and long (>10 min up to 1 h) "critical periods". (Guido Vroemen & Marco van Bon, 2019) — [Should You Be Evaluating Your Cyclists Using Critical Periods?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/should-you-be-evaluating-your-cyclists-using-critical-periods/)
- Heuristic mapping used "for almost 20 years": "20-minute power can be used to estimate threshold, five-minute power is a good indication of VO2max or maximum aerobic power, one-minute power is related to anaerobic ability, and five-second power tells you if an athlete is a good sprinter." (Cody Stephenson, Dec 2025) — [The Power Duration Curve: A Deep Dive Into Modeled Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/power-duration-curve-modeled-power/) **[outside Science listing]**
- Other listed bike protocols (TP athlete blog, Dave Schell, 2018, upd. 2026): 20-min TT "subtract 5%"; 30-min TT use full average, no subtraction; CP from a short (3–5 min) + long (20–30 min) effort, CP = (AP1×T1 − AP2×T2)/(T2 − T1); 8-minute test = two 8-min efforts, subtract 10 % from the highest; ramp test (increase watts each minute to failure); 3-min all-out CP = average of last 30 s; mFTP from the PD curve. — [Threshold Tests for Swim, Bike and Run](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/threshold-tests-for-swim-bike-and-run/) **[outside Science category]**

**Bike — Critical Power / W′ and modeled power (mFTP, FRC, Pmax)**
- CP "represents the asymptote of the power-duration curve… Above CP, fatigue develops predictably, and exhaustion occurs following depletion of W′. Below CP, physiological variables can stabilise… Modern CP testing typically uses multiple maximal efforts between 2-15 minutes, allowing both CP and W′ to be modelled simultaneously." "In many athletes, CP is higher than FTP" and studies "found poor agreement between the two metrics and that they should not be used interchangeably (Karsten et al., 2021)". (2026) — [FTP vs. Critical Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/ftp-vs-critical-power/)
- "If CP is higher than FTP, workouts based on CP may place threshold and VO2max intervals at higher absolute intensities than an FTP-based model." With CP "you also have the W′ component… you can instead individualise the workload of each athlete to target a certain percentage of depletion". (2026) — same URL
- TrainingPeaks' modeled power (Analyze 360) fits three parameters to ~90 days of data: **Pmax** (sprint), **mFTP** ("can be interpreted the same way as FTP or other threshold tests… If an athlete sets a new peak power for 47 minutes and 11 seconds in a race or a new 18:03 best chasing a KOM, their mFTP will update"), **FRC** ("amount of work an athlete can do above their threshold continuously… spans the critical duration range from about one minute to around eight minutes"). "Some athletes have high anaerobic energy contribution to 20-minute power tests… so those tests overestimate their FTP and require customized adjustment factors." (Dec 2025) — [PD Curve: Modeled Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/power-duration-curve-modeled-power/)
- mFTP is framed as "a modern descendant of both FTP and CP"; FRC ≈ W′ analogue. (2026) — [FTP vs. Critical Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/ftp-vs-critical-power/)

**Bike — Mean-max power (MMP) curve as a testing substitute**
- TP power curve highlights "5, 10, 12, 20, and 30 seconds, along with 1, 2, 5, 6, 10, 12, 20, 30, 60, 90, and 180 minutes". "Ninety days is generally accepted as a valid period to judge an athlete's current abilities without requiring new tests… Beyond 90 days, changes in conditioning may mean a new effort is needed to verify current capacity." Energy-system bands: "1 to 15–30 seconds: ATP-CP; 30 seconds to 4–8 minutes: glycolytic; Beyond 8 minutes: aerobic." Phenotypes: sprinter, pursuiter, all-rounder, time trialist ("flatter curve with strong long-duration power… suited to time trials, long-course triathlon"). (Cody Stephenson, Nov 2025) — [All About the Mean Max Power Curve](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/all-about-the-mean-max-power-curve/)
- GAP-analysis example: athlete with 5/10/20-min PBs of 324/294/274 W → coach substitutes a **10-min test "at regular intervals… perhaps after each 3-week cycle"** for the 20-min test once the 10↔20-min relationship is known; re-assess after a 6–8-week Zone-2 block. (Tom Epton) — [How Coaches Can Use GAP Analyses](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-coaches-can-use-gap-analyses-to-guide-training/) **[outside Science listing]**

**Lactate testing (bike & run) — LT1/LT2 protocol**
- Protocol: "Start very easy… A common approach is +20–30 watts every 5–8 minutes for cycling, taking a lactate sample at the end of each stage. For running, use small pace increases on the same time structure." "plan stages of at least five minutes" because lactate lags ("1.5 mmol/L at 300W, then… the same 1.5 mmol/L two minutes later… Hold that same intensity for five minutes (or longer), and the number may climb"). Use ERG-mode smart trainer / treadmill; record pace-or-power + HR on the athlete's own devices. (Tom Epton, pub. Jan 2023, upd. Mar 2026) — [A Coach's Guide to Lactate Testing](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lactate-threshold-testing-better-performance/)
- Interpretation: resting lactate "0–2 mmol/L"; sharper rise "somewhere around 4–4.5 mmol/L, but the exact number varies"; **AeT/LT1** = "first intensity where lactate rises meaningfully above baseline"; **AnT/LT2** = where "lactate begins to rise rapidly… often aligns with the highest intensity an athlete can sustain for roughly 30–60 minutes… In cycling, it often maps closely to FTP for many athletes." LT2 work: "95–105% of LT2 using a 2:1 work-to-rest ratio". (2023/2026) — same URL
- Sport specificity: "Lactate numbers do not transfer cleanly from one discipline to another… For triathletes, best practice is to test and interpret lactate separately for the bike and the run, then set discipline-specific zones… it can even make sense to test on different bike setups (road vs tri position)". (2023/2026) — same URL
- Device consistency: "If an athlete tests on a lab ergometer that reads 5% higher than their power meter, then builds zones off those lab numbers, the training prescription will be off from day one. That often shows up as workouts that feel harder than expected, chronic missed targets, and unnecessary fatigue." (2023/2026) — same URL
- Curve shift: base training "shift[s] the curve to the right"; after "a long break or detraining period, the curve often shifts left". Peak lactate "can also be lower when an athlete is carrying excessive fatigue or showing signs consistent with overreaching." (2023/2026) — same URL
- Cost benchmark for lab tests: "$99 to $300 per sport" (Simmons, 2020) — [Zone Setting Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/); "a lab test often costs about half the price of buying your own blood lactate analyzer" (Epton, 2023/2026).

**VO2max / MAP field tests**
- Sandford protocol for the speed–endurance continuum: **MSS** = 50 m sprint (run) or 10-s all-out (bike, best 3-s average); **MAS/MAP** = "a six-minute running or cycling effort. This approach demonstrates a strong correlation, with a coefficient of around 0.96 or 0.97, closely mirroring the velocity of VO2 max recorded in laboratory settings." (Tom Epton, Jan 2024) — [Speed v. Endurance](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/speed-v-endurance/)
- Ramp test in practice: coaches "use the built-in testing protocol on the Wattbike to perform the ramp test… one-minute stages that progressively get harder until they can't go anymore and gives a predicted VO2 max if they were able to go through a minimum of five stages", plus "a five-minute max aerobic power test… and what their heart rate recovery is like a minute later." (Phil White, 2023) — [Coaching 60+ Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-athletes-in-60s-endurance-sports/)
- Watch VO2max estimates: anthropometrics-only models R² ≈ 0.54; adding resting HR → 0.59; HRV adds nothing; adding sub-maximal running HR → R² 0.67, SE 4.1 ml/kg/min; estimate correlates with 10K–marathon performance at population level, but within-individual tracking was still open. (Marco Altini, 2017) — [What Can I Really Do With a VO2max Estimate?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/vo2max-estimates-workouts-can-tell-us/)
- VO2max session targeting is about physiology not zone: field proxies "HR ramps over the first 2–4 reps and stays high", "dominant breathing stress", "Power is consistent across reps"; classic 4×4/3×5 at "115%+ FTP" for ceiling-limited athletes; 30/15s for kinetics-limited. (Taylor Warren, Feb 2026) — [Stop Prescribing Zone 5](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/stop-prescribing-zone-5/) **[outside Science listing]**

**Run — pace / HR tests**
- Standard: "5K, 8K, 10K Baseline Test / Time Trial… standard protocol across all running coaches… Longer protocols are best for determining fitness for Half Marathon to Marathon… can use increasing decoupling to determine when they begin running inefficiently. Take note to use these sparingly." Sub-maximal proxies: **5×1 km on 400 m jog** "seven days out from a 5K fitness test… Take the average of five reps and multiply by five to get the target time/pace"; **Yasso 800s** (8×800 on 2:00, minutes→hours for marathon). (Simmons, 2020/2026) — [Zone Setting Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/)
- Friel 30-min test (TP Learn, pub. 2012, upd. 2026): "Do a 30-minute time trial all by yourself… as if it was a race"; **LTHR = average HR of the last 20 min** (lap at 10 min); **run threshold pace = average pace for the entire 30 min (not the last 20)**; **bike FTP = average power for the entire 30 min**. — [Joe Friel's Quick Guide to Setting Zones](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/) **[outside Science category]**
- Other run options (Schell, 2018/2026): 5K/10K race → TP threshold; Critical Velocity from 3-min + 9-min efforts; "7 out of 10 [RPE] is highly correlated with MLSS". — [Threshold Tests for Swim, Bike and Run](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/threshold-tests-for-swim-bike-and-run/) **[outside Science category]**
- Marathon prescription: coach uses "pace zones of .91 to .95 for marathon training"; watches "Sixty-minute speed month-to-month" for marathoners and 20-min speed for 10K athletes as fitness markers. (Andrew Simmons) — [3 Things Your Athletes' Running Data Could Be Telling You](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/athletes-running-data/) (in Science listing)
- Trail/mountain: "threshold testing is rarely a good indication of race readiness… Regularly monitoring an athlete's average HR on a known ascent or descent serves as a better indicator". (Simmons, 2020/2023) — [The Fitness Testing We Should Be Doing](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fitness-testing-we-should-be-doing/)

**Swim — T-pace / CSS**
- "One of the more common tests entails swimming **3X300 with 30 seconds of rest** at the best possible average pace. The average pace per 100 is an athlete's threshold, or T-pace (Zone 4)." Threshold sets = "100- to 200-meter repeats at T-pace on short rest"; endurance = "300- to 800-meter repeats on short rest". (Conrad Goeringer, pub. 2019, upd. 2026) — [5 Must-Do Off-Season Triathlon Swim Training Tips](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/5-must-do-off-season-triathlon-swim-training-tips/)
- Swim TT variants (from Bernhardt & Hansen via coach Nadja Tapkas): **3×100 on 20 s rest** ("no more than a 5-second difference between each one"); **3×300 on 30 s** ("all three 300's should be within 15 seconds of each other"); **Broken 1k** = 400 (30″) + 300 (20″) + 200 (10″) + 100 best effort, "total time of the main set minus 1:00 to estimate your 1K swim time". (Simmons, 2020/2026) — [Zone Setting Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/)
- Category mentions CSS only in passing: "Athletes can use a Tempo Trainer to increase stroke rate for CSS/race pace-type workouts" (race planning, 2022) — [Race Planning a Triathlon Using Science](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/race-planning-triathlon-using-science/). The formula lives outside the category: "CSS/100m = (T(400)-T(200))/2… 400 in 6 minutes and the 200 in 3 minutes → 90 seconds per 100"; 1,000 m TT ÷ 10 = threshold 100 pace; 400 m TT "Add 4-5 seconds to 100m pace for CSS estimate, add 8-10 seconds for Endurance pace". (Schell, 2018/2026) — [Threshold Tests for Swim, Bike and Run](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/threshold-tests-for-swim-bike-and-run/); Friel: "Swim a 1000-meter/yard time trial… Divide finish time by 10 to determine T-time". — [Friel Quick Guide](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/)

**Zone models referenced in the category**
- Base-season article recommends "Joe Friel's heart rate zones and Andy Coggan's power zones" together; per-zone durations: Z2 "for hours"; Z3 "five to ten minutes and up to over 30 minutes"; Z4 "up to 15-minute periods or longer"; Z5+ "short efforts up to several minutes". (Mike Schultz, pub. 2019, upd. 2026) — [How to Build Zone-Based Workouts in the Off-Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-build-zone-based-workouts-in-the-off-season/)
- %HRmax 5-zone table shown (Z1 50–60 %, Z2 60–70 %, Z3 70–80 %, Z4 80–90 %, Z5 90–100 %) in a general HR article (Phil White, 2021, upd. 2025) — [Does it Matter if Your Athletes Have High Resting Heart Rates?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/does-it-matter-if-your-athletes-have-high-resting-and-working-heart-rates/)
- Friel zone tables (outside category, 2012/2026): Run LTHR Z1 <85 %, Z2 85–89, Z3 90–94, Z4 95–99, Z5a 100–102, Z5b 103–106, Z5c >106 %; Bike LTHR Z1 <81 %, Z2 81–89, Z3 90–93, Z4 94–99, Z5a 100–102, Z5b 103–106, Z5c >106 %; Bike power Z1 <55 % FTP, Z2 55–74, Z3 75–89, Z4 90–104, Z5 105–120, Z6 >120 %; Run pace Z1 >129 % of threshold pace (slower), Z2 114–129, Z3 106–113, Z4 99–105, Z5a 97–100, Z5b 90–96, Z5c <90 % (faster); swim zones = "T-time plus or minus a few seconds". — [Friel Quick Guide](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/joe-friel-s-quick-guide-to-setting-zones/)
- TP's zone calculator: "The calculations offered for each sport are backed by supporting research… or you can create custom zones". Devices do not auto-update: "devices do not change zones when the coach changes them in TrainingPeaks. If your athlete is finding a mismatch, they have not updated their device". (Simmons, 2020/2026) — [Zone Setting Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/)

### Inferences
- A defensible protocol set consistent with the category: **Bike** — prefer mFTP/90-day MMP where ≥90 days of maximal efforts exist; otherwise 20-min × 0.95 (or full 30-min average, or 60-min/40 km TT), and treat 0.95 as an athlete-specific factor to be adjusted if the athlete is anaerobically strong (factor lower) or diesel (factor higher); add 5-min MAP and 5–15 s sprint for the profile. **Run** — 5K/10K race or 30-min solo TT (pace = whole 30 min; LTHR = last 20 min), with 5×1 km or 10-min tests as low-cost interim checks. **Swim** — 3×300 (30″) average/100 as T-pace, cross-checked against 400/200 CSS or 1000 TT ÷ 10.
- Lactate LT2 ≈ FTP ≈ 30–60-min sustainable is the expected relationship; CP is typically a bit higher than FTP, so zones built off CP should be labelled as such and not mixed with FTP-based zones.
- The 90-day window in the MMP article is effectively TrainingPeaks' definition of "current" — a best older than 90 days should be re-verified.

### Gaps
- No Science-category article gives the CSS formula or a run-power test protocol; those come from TP Learn/athlete-blog pieces (cited above and flagged).
- No Science article quantifies typical FTP↔CP or 20-min↔60-min gaps for triathletes; the 0.95 factor is only stated (2020, 2026) and criticised (2026), never replaced with a triathlon-specific value.
- No article in the category discusses ramp-test correction factors (e.g. % of 1-min max ramp power); the "ramp test" mention (2023) is a Wattbike VO2max predictor, not an FTP test.

## Key Question 2 — How should thresholds be set for HR vs power vs pace, and what drift do the articles expect with heat and fatigue?

### Takeaway
The category treats power/pace as the prescribing signals and HR as the lagging cross-check ("gauge zones by at least two of the three"), quantifies fatigue/heat via aerobic decoupling (Pa:Hr / Pw:Hr, <5 % good, >10 % above AeT or under-trained), and expects sleep loss and heat to raise HR and lactate at a given power without necessarily changing max output.

### Cited Findings
- HR vs power in Zone 4: "you will start to see a greater disconnect between heart rate and power… If aerobically fatigued, heart rate will lag even if you can reach the zone with power immediately. If fresh, heart rate may drift higher into Zone 5 while in Zone 4 with power… A good rule of thumb is to gauge zones by at least two of the three sources of feedback [power, HR, RPE]." Z2: "If either power or heart rate is low and perceived exertion is high, that is a good sign to back off". Z5+: "This is where power becomes the most precise tool". (Mike Schultz, 2019/2026) — [Zone-Based Workouts in the Off-Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-build-zone-based-workouts-in-the-off-season/)
- Aerobic decoupling (Joe Friel, pub. 2008, upd. 2026): TP "compar[es] the first half of a steady effort to the second half"; "< 5% decoupling: Strong aerobic endurance at that intensity; 5–10%: Moderate endurance limitations or fatigue; > 10%: The effort was likely above aerobic threshold or the athlete lacks the endurance". Causes of drift: "rising core temperature, dehydration, glycogen depletion, and cardiovascular fatigue". High decoupling may indicate "intensity was too high… lacks sufficient aerobic base… Environmental stress (heat, dehydration)… accumulated fatigue". Durability benchmarks: cycling "two to four hours at aerobic threshold (AeT)"; running "one to two hours at AeT"; "Over time, you should see heart rate drift decrease at the same intensity… often a clearer indicator of aerobic development than single test results." — [Aerobic Decoupling and Heart Rate Drift Explained](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/) **[outside Science listing]**
- Running coach's decoupling use: "Within the 0-5% range, we're in a happy place"; example 5K test gone out too hard → "fades in the final 1000-meters while their HR is pegged"; a rep with 8.87 % decoupling in the first 4 min vs 1.47 % for the remaining 8 min flagged "starting off too fast". (Simmons) — [3 Things Your Athletes' Running Data Could Be Telling You](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/athletes-running-data/)
- Sleep loss (Phil White, 2023): after delayed bedtime/early awakening "blood lactate was higher during submaximal exercise" in a 30-min TT, so athletes "might hit their lactate threshold sooner than usual" in moderate sessions ≥30 min, while "red-line output may be unaffected"; good sleepers show "higher values of Wmax, VO2max and lower values of HRmax"; power-to-HR ratio "stayed consistent" when rested; "Intensity ratios incorporating mean HR seem sensitive to effects of sleep duration"; 4 h vs 8 h sleep → lower 12-min test distance, higher RPE, altered core temperature. Practical: log RPE; "If you notice a trend indicating that they're finding a tempo run, lactate threshold ride… harder than usual, it's a signal to ask more questions." — [Impact of Sleep Loss on Performance Physiology](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/impact-sleep-loss-performance-physiology/)
- Lactate curve under fatigue: peak lactate "can also be lower when an athlete is carrying excessive fatigue or showing signs consistent with overreaching" (Epton 2023/2026) — [Lactate Testing](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lactate-threshold-testing-better-performance/)
- HR as lagging signal: track 1 km reps where "heart rate rose steadily… then settled around 178bpm" while "power remained steady throughout"; in a hilly XC race "peak 30-minute heart rate of 192 bpm — around my threshold" although pace said Z2–3; "Heart rate is a lagging indicator of effort, but power is instantaneous… Using a combination of power and heart rate to measure effort on a hilly course will often be superior to pace." (Epton, 2023/2026) — [Is Coaching With Running Power Better Than Pace or HR?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-running-power-better/)
- Heat and biochemical efficiency: "an athlete could test at a given power, heart rate and core temperature to establish a baseline. The athlete would then do a heat tolerance block to train the body to adapt and generate more watts for the same heart rate and core temperature." (Epton, 2023) — [Human Efficiency and Triathlon](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/human-efficiency-impact-triathlon-achievement/)
- Analyze 360 layers "heat strain, breathing, and sweat metrics, on top of power, pace" (TrainingPeaks, Feb 2026) — [Analyze 360](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/see-and-analyze-every-metric/)
- Indoor/virtual: "indoor racing feels harder—even if you're hitting the same power and pace" (Justin Ross, 2020) — [Why Virtual Racing Hurts So Much](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/why-virtual-racing-hurts-so-much/) (perception, not physiology; the category gives no indoor FTP correction — see Gaps)
- Normalized Power caveats for interpreting threshold work: "coaches are taught to dismiss NP values for durations under 10 minutes… The 30-second window should be less than or equal to 5% of the overall interval"; "Average power is more appropriate than NP on long climbs or during time trials. NP should not be used as a measure of judging performance"; NP "may underestimate the load on an athlete new to the sport but overestimate the load on a very well-trained athlete." (Epton, 2022/2024) — [Understanding Normalized Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/normalized-power-how-coaches-use/)
- RPE anchoring: Threshold ≈ RPE "5-6" on the article's 10-point table; PE "might differ from others during workouts… that require similar relative effort levels (e.g., percentage of FTP)". (Fabiano Araujo, 2023) — [Why RPE Is One of the Most Valuable Metrics](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/basis-of-rpe-why-coaches-should-use-it/)
- Durability: "a rider's durability in longer endurance events (~90 min+) is a key performance indicator"; TP predicts kJ from "% of FTP" structured workouts; kJ ≈ kcal 1:1 because 4.184 and ~22.5 % human efficiency cancel. (Matti Rowe, 2024) — [Predicting and Measuring Kilojoules](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/predicting-measuring-kilojoules-trainingpeaks/). "If the rider loses 10% of their five-minute power after a particular workload, that's useful information"; kJ alone "is limited as a marker of fatigue because the intensity at which the work is accumulated matters" (2026 systematic review cited). — [What Durability Data Can't Measure](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-durability-data-can-not-measure/) **[outside Science listing]**

### Inferences
- Rule for a coaching assistant: set the *prescribing* threshold in the unit the athlete trains by (power on bike, pace or power on run, pace in swim), set LTHR separately from the same or a parallel test, and use Pw:Hr / Pa:Hr plus RPE to decide whether a mismatch is fatigue/heat (HR high for the same power, decoupling >5 %) or a stale threshold (HR *and* RPE low for target power).
- Expected direction of drift from these sources: heat, dehydration, poor sleep and glycogen depletion push HR and lactate up at fixed power/pace (i.e. the HR threshold appears reached earlier), whereas the power/pace threshold itself may be unchanged in short maximal efforts — so a heat-affected 20-min test underestimates FTP while an LTHR from the same test may be roughly valid.

### Gaps
- No Science-category article gives numeric heat corrections (e.g. % FTP loss per °C, or indoor-vs-outdoor FTP factor); TP has an athlete-blog piece "Adjusting Your FTP for Indoor Riding" that was not fetched.

## Key Question 3 — What do the articles say about run power thresholds and platform differences (Stryd vs Garmin/Coros)?

### Takeaway
The category endorses run power as an instantaneous, terrain-independent prescribing signal (with efficiency = speed/power as the key derived metric), names Stryd as the validated "gold standard" and wrist/watch power as adequate only for rough consistency checks, and warns that device-native power silently inflates/deflates TSS until a device-specific run-power threshold is set — but it never publishes a cross-platform conversion factor.

### Cited Findings
- Device hierarchy: "wrist-based power meters are sufficient in some use cases, but if you want 'true' values, a foot pod is ideal… A foot pod is also required if you're prescribing sessions by power or pacing a race. Watch-based data is good enough for roughly checking consistency across intervals". "Validation studies on any power measurement devices other than those made by Stryd currently need to be improved… for now, the Stryd is the gold standard." (Tom Epton, pub. 2023, upd. Apr 2026) — [Is Coaching With Running Power Better Than Pace or HR?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-running-power-better/)
- TSS symptom of wrong platform/threshold: "If you see TSS values for running workouts that are unrealistically high or low for the perceived effort or compared to recent similar workouts, your device may be recording running power, and you will need to adjust your zone settings accordingly. Many new devices have running power automatically turned on." (2023/2026) — same URL; TP Help Center article "Why do my running TSS values seem wrong?" exists on this topic (https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/13293242078989-Why-do-my-running-TSS-values-seem-wrong) — surfaced by search, not fetched.
- "coaches must ensure their athlete has accurate and current thresholds through regular testing. This is even more important with running than cycling due to the greater injury risk." (2023/2026) — same URL
- Efficiency metric: "Speed (m/s) = Running Effectiveness (Kg/N) x Power (W/Kg)" (Palladino); triathletes who struggle off the bike do so "because of a reduced efficiency factor, but their power values remain largely unaffected. Therefore, racing triathlon becomes a simple case of targeting a power number based on what they can sustain for the duration of the race." (Philip Hatzis, pub. 2020, upd. 2026) — [Is Running with Power Worth It?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/is-running-with-power-worth-it/)
- Jim Vance's 2016 Ali'i Drive example (same pace & HR on two days, higher power on day 2 after a bike crash → athlete "less effective") — same URL
- Zone-setting workflow: "a runner sets power-based training zones. This process is similar to establishing a set of pace-based zones or heart-rate zones"; workouts pushed to Garmin or Apple Watch via Stryd app; power lets the athlete "accurately maintain their effort in an easy zone" on hills where HR lags. (Frank Campo, 2019) — [How To Use Run Power For Race-Specific Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-use-run-power-for-race-specific-training/)
- Metabolic vs mechanical: "in running, it's still kind of a little hazy as to whether we're trying to determine metabolic power or mechanical power… at the fundamental level, it doesn't really matter because… running power is merely just a means to control our energy across a distance or duration." Cycling analogy: "300 watts you can do for 20 minutes… we know that 95% of that's probably going to be around your anaerobic threshold." Hills: "you could be pushing into Zone 4 or, say, 100-105% of your power threshold. Pace looks fine, the heart rate's drifting up… running a hill at Zone 4+ power is costing [you] anaerobic work." (Will O'Connor / Dirk Friel podcast, Apr 2024) — [How to Utilize Running Power Metrics](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/utilize-running-power-metrics-will-oconnor/) **[outside Science listing]**
- WKO run-power tempo example: marathoner's 2×25 min "at ~92 to 94 percent of FTP" (run power), second-half power +4.6 %, speed +10 %, Running Effectiveness +6.1 %, LSS +0.8 %. (2017) — [Using WKO4 to Analyze the Struggle in a Run Workout](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/using-wko4-to-analyze-the-struggle-in-a-run-workout/)
- Leg spring stiffness as an economy proxy (Hans van Dijk, 2019) — [LSS and Running Economy](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/using-leg-spring-stiffness-to-measure-running-economy/) (not threshold-specific)
- rFTPw protocol (Jim Vance, TP Learn, pub. 2016, upd. 2026): 15-min warm-up → **3-min max** → 30-min structured recovery → **9-min max** → cool-down; "take the average power for the 3 minutes and the 9 minutes, and average the two values… take 90 percent of that value, which is within about +/-3 percent of your actual value"; example (350 + 300)/2 × 0.90 = 293 W. Vance: "NOT to set zones at first" because cycling zones "don't apply with run power meters"; article names Stryd and RPM2 but gives no cross-device comparison. — [Running With Power: How to Find Your Run FTP](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/learn/articles/running-with-power-how-to-find-your-run-ftp/) **[outside Science category]**
- TP offers run-power zones "based on Matt Fitzgerald's 80/20 Endurance system" (TP Help Center, surfaced by search: https://help.trainingpeaks.com/hc/en-us/articles/360017420092-Zones-Calculator-Overview) — not fetched.

### Inferences
- Because the category only says Garmin/Coros values are less validated and produce "unrealistically high or low" TSS, the safe operational rule is: **keep a separate run-power threshold per device/ecosystem, never compare watts across Stryd and Garmin/Coros, and re-test (rFTPw or 30-min/race-based) when the athlete changes device or firmware**. Any numeric Garmin→Stryd scaling would have to come from outside TrainingPeaks.
- Run-power "threshold" in TP usage ≈ 90 % of the 3-min/9-min average (Vance) or ≈ the 30–60-min sustainable power; the 92–94 % FTP marathon-tempo example (2017) is consistent with pace-based 0.91–0.95 marathon zones (Simmons).

### Gaps
- **No TrainingPeaks article found (in or outside the category) gives a quantitative Garmin-vs-Stryd scale difference or conversion factor.** The only guidance is qualitative (Stryd validated; watch power OK for consistency checks; fix TSS by setting a device-appropriate threshold).
- Vance's promised run-power zone article was not located in this category.

## Key Question 4 — How frequently should thresholds be updated, and what symptoms indicate they are wrong or stale?

### Takeaway
The category's own guidance is qualitative — retest often enough to catch adaptation but not so often as to burn athletes out, review after every block/major disruption, and lean on continuously updated proxies (90-day MMP/mFTP, "Thresholds Over Time", decoupling, IF and HR review) — while the TP athlete blog gives the numbers (every 30 days, or every 6–8 weeks, or a few times per season).

### Cited Findings
- Frequency paradox: "The paradox of testing is that it should be done as frequently as to determine physiological improvement but as infrequently as to avoid significant mental tax and burnout." "assure the athletes rest adequately in the days leading up to testing." "Critical power tests are extremely intense, so use these sparingly throughout the season." (Simmons, 2020/2026) — [Zone Setting Deep Dive](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-zone-setting-and-testing-deep-dive/)
- Regression is not flagged automatically: "TrainingPeaks is set up to remind you of increases in Threshold but does not indicate a regression; to ensure you've got an accurate picture of the athlete, make sure you're reviewing their IF and HR regularly as part of your feedback assessment." Also "look into your athletes' zones during the off-season". (2020/2026) — same URL
- Device sync symptom: "devices do not change zones when the coach changes them in TrainingPeaks. If your athlete is finding a mismatch, they have not updated their device". (2020/2026) — same URL
- Lactate cadence: "A single lactate test provides a snapshot of fitness, but the real value comes from seeing how thresholds evolve across training blocks. The Thresholds Over Time feature in TrainingPeaks allows coaches to track historical values". Stale-zone symptom (from device mismatch or wrong threshold): "workouts that feel harder than expected, chronic missed targets, and unnecessary fatigue." (Epton, 2023/2026) — [Lactate Testing](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lactate-threshold-testing-better-performance/)
- 90-day rule: "If an athlete has recorded a best performance within that period, it is reasonable to assume they can repeat it with similar fitness. Beyond 90 days… a new effort is needed to verify current capacity." "If an athlete has not recently completed maximal efforts across these time domains, the curve may be incomplete… the 90-day curve becomes a roadmap for identifying gaps and planning testing." (Stephenson, Nov 2025) — [MMP Curve](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/all-about-the-mean-max-power-curve/)
- mFTP "reduces the need for specific, structured testing, as all new data contributes to mFTP"; example where mFTP fell in-season while FRC rose and results improved — "seeing that decline… may have unnecessarily created doubt or panic". (Dec 2025) — [PD Curve: Modeled Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/power-duration-curve-modeled-power/)
- Re-assessment after blocks: 10-min test "perhaps after each 3-week cycle"; re-assess power or metabolic profile after a "6-8 weeks" Zone-2 block. (Epton) — [GAP Analyses](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-coaches-can-use-gap-analyses-to-guide-training/)
- Run-power: "coaches must ensure their athlete has accurate and current thresholds through regular testing" (Epton 2023/2026) — [Coaching With Running Power](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-running-power-better/)
- A lower test number isn't automatically lost fitness: "When you take a fitness test and FTP goes down, it's very easy to assume that you've lost fitness… Assessing your fitness by only using Threshold Pace, FTP, or 100m pace average is a dangerous premise." (Simmons, 2020/2023) — [The Fitness Testing We Should Be Doing](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fitness-testing-we-should-be-doing/)
- Symptoms a threshold/zone is wrong (VO2 context): "If power is high but HR/ventilation isn't: shorten recoveries, lengthen the set…"; "Athlete needs days to recover (you likely turned it into an anaerobic smashfest)"; "Huge first rep, then dramatic fade". (Warren, Feb 2026) — [Stop Prescribing Zone 5](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/stop-prescribing-zone-5/)
- Fatigue vs stale threshold via decoupling: "persistent heart rate drift may signal that the athlete needs more aerobic volume"; low drift over 90 min → ready to extend/intensify. (Friel 2008/2026) — [Aerobic Decoupling](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/aerobic-endurance-and-decoupling/)
- Readiness before testing: use HRV, resting HR, mood, blood values; some pros take "no time" off, others "up to six weeks" (Epton, 2022) — [Determining When Your Athletes Are Prepared to Train Again](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/determining-when-athletes-train-again/)
- Numeric cadence (TP athlete blog, Mike Ricci, pub. 2021, upd. 2026): "You should retest FTP every thirty days. You should also retest it after any major event that affects training" (improved fitness, injury, busy work schedule, time away). Low result → "try the test again in a week or two"; if still declining "it's time to adjust your zones… often indicate that it's time to back off!" High result → confirm "you have plenty of time in that range during the intervals leading up to your test", otherwise "hold off until your next test. Prematurely raising your FTP can generate too much fatigue and put you at risk of injury." — [Is It Time to Reset Your FTP? 3 Signs](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/is-it-time-to-reset-your-ftp/) **[outside Science category]**
- Other TP guidance surfaced by search (not fetched): "every six to eight weeks during training", and "a few times per year (baseline, partway through pre-competition, in-season peak)" with more frequent testing for beginners/returning athletes — [The Athlete's Guide to FTP](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/the-athlete-guide-to-ftp/) / [What Is Functional Threshold Power?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/what-is-threshold-power/) **[outside category; snippets only]**
- Threshold-improvement notifications exist for HR in TP (surfaced by search, not fetched): [Using Heart Rate Threshold Notifications](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/blog/are-you-using-threshold-improvement-notifications/)

### Inferences
- A workable rule set for an assistant, consistent with the sources: (1) formal test or race-derived update per sport every 4–8 weeks in build phases, always at the end of a recovery week; (2) automatic re-check whenever the 90-day MMP/mFTP or a race produces a new best at 20–60 min (bike) or 5K–10K (run) or 400–1000 m (swim); (3) mandatory re-test after ≥2–3 weeks off, illness/injury, device change (especially run power), or a season phase change; (4) between tests, watch IF of steady sessions, Pw:Hr/Pa:Hr, HR-at-target-power and RPE.
- Symptom matrix implied by the sources: **threshold too high** → chronic missed targets, workouts feel harder than expected, HR at/above threshold on "tempo" work, decoupling >10 % on supposedly aerobic sessions, high RPE with normal or low power, multi-day recovery need; **threshold too low** → IF > ~1.0 on long steady efforts, HR and RPE low at Z4 power, easy "new bests" in training, TSS/CTL inflating without felt strain; **device/platform artefact** → run TSS wildly off vs RPE, zones mismatched between device and TP.

### Gaps
- The Science category itself never states a numeric retest interval; the 30-day / 6–8-week / few-per-season figures all come from TP athlete-blog articles (flagged).
- No article gives numeric IF/TSS thresholds for detecting a stale FTP (e.g. "IF > 1.05 for 60 min means FTP is too low"); that heuristic is inference only.
- No swim-specific retest cadence found anywhere on TrainingPeaks in this pass (only "At the beginning and throughout the off-season swim block").
