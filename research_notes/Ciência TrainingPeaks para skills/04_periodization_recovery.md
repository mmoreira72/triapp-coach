# TrainingPeaks Coach Blog "Science" category: periodization, intensity distribution and recovery monitoring

Scope and method. The "Science" category page (https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/category/science/) is implemented as WordPress tag id 117 ("science") inside the "coaching" category and paginates via an AJAX "load more" endpoint (24 nominal pages, 10 posts each; 19 pages actually return content). Enumerating every page yielded 233 unique articles (as of 2026-09-30). All 233 were downloaded and keyword-searched; 62 relevant to periodization, intensity, strength, ageing and recovery monitoring were read in full. Important coverage caveat: the category contains NO articles by Stephen Seiler, Joe Friel or Alan Couzens, and no article whose subject is polarized vs pyramidal vs sweet-spot distribution or block periodization as such. The word "polarized" appears in only two of the 233 articles (once in passing). The 80/20 article by Matt Fitzgerald is linked as "related" from a Science article but lives in the athlete blog, outside this category. Most recovery-monitoring content comes from Simon Wegerif (founder of the ithlete HRV app, 2009-2025; a commercial interest worth noting). Publication dates run 2014-2026; many pages show an "Updated 2025/2026" stamp.

## Key question 1: What intensity distribution and weekly structures do the articles recommend, with numbers?

### Takeaway
The category offers no explicit polarized/pyramidal prescription; the only quantified intensity distributions are a research example (roughly 65/25/10 percent low/moderate/high by time in a cycling study) and a coaching rule that ~90% of workouts should feel RPE 5-7 with only ~9% at RPE 8-9. Weekly-structure guidance is expressed instead in TrainingPeaks load terms (TSS/CTL/TSB): keep TSB above about -30, CTL ramp rate 5-7 points per week, avoid load jumps above 10%, plan a recovery week every third or fourth week, base weeks on aerobic volume first and add intensity into the remaining TSS budget, and stretch the cycle to 9-10 days for masters or injury-prone athletes.

### Cited Findings
- Intensity distribution numbers (research, not a prescription): in the HRV-guided cycling study reviewed by Wegerif, the HRV-guided group's time distribution was 66/24/10 percent low/moderate/high, the traditional group 64/27/9; both averaged ~9 h/week training and ~8 h/week during baseline; 17 well-trained cyclists, mean age 39, ~220 W 40-min TT power — [New Study Widens HRV Evidence for More Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/new-study-widens-hrv-evidence-for-more-athletes/)
- The Kiviniemi HRV-guided algorithm alternates "low" vs "high" intensity running "in a simple polarized running training program" stepped daily by morning HRV — [Is HRV Training the Best Approach for Athletes?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hrv-guided-training/)
- Effort distribution by RPE: "We want fives, sixes, and sevens for 90 percent of our workouts. Another nine percent will be eights and nines, with competition as a ten"; you should not be so tired from the last session that you dread the next — [Off-Season Training: When It's Time to Ditch the Trainer](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/off-season-training-when-its-time-to-ditch-the-trainer/)
- Unstructured outdoor winter rides: at most 3 such rides in a 7-day window; most should end with IF 0.60-0.75 and, for a typical 70.3 age-grouper or Cat 3, TSS 150-225 per ride — [Off-Season Training: When It's Time to Ditch the Trainer](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/off-season-training-when-its-time-to-ditch-the-trainer/)
- Load-planning guardrails: "generally avoid a TSB of greater than -30, keep ramp rate to five to seven points of CTL, and minimize overloading in any direction more than 10%"; Annual Training Plan includes a recovery cycle "every third or fourth week"; build the week's aerobic base sessions and long sessions first, then distribute the remaining TSS to high-intensity work; strength sessions are NOT counted in TSS so total load must be judged with RPE/subjective feedback — [How To Design An Endurance Athlete's Total Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/designing-an-athletes-total-load/)
- TSS anchors: a 1-h all-out 40 km TT ~100 TSS; 1 h moderate ~50 TSS; five rides of ~67-101 TSS in 6-8 h/week yields a CTL near 60; a Cat-level cyclocross winner averaged 8.25 h/week training plus racing — [Maximizing Athlete Fitness on Limited Time](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/maximizing-athlete-fitness-on-limited-time/)
- Plateau reference: "For most amateur athletes, this point [where further training won't lead to improvements] starts around 110 CTL" — [The 4 Signs of Overtraining](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- Base/build zone use (Friel HR zones, Coggan power zones): Zone 1 = recovery days after races or inside recovery weeks; Zone 2 sustainable for hours, capillary density and fat oxidation; Zone 3 "sweet spot" efforts of 5-10 min up to 30+ min spaced with Zone 2; Zone 4 sustainable ~15 min, race-specific, belongs in build; Zone 5+ "sparingly" in base, short efforts of up to several minutes; "Focus on longer Zone 2 and 3 spin efforts for your hard workouts early in the base season. As you progress into build periods, work more Zone 4 and 5 efforts" — [How to Build Zone-Based Workouts in the Off-Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-build-zone-based-workouts-in-the-off-season/)
- Rule for zone verification: gauge zones by at least two of three feedback sources (power, HR, RPE); if power or HR is low and RPE is high, back off for the day — [How to Build Zone-Based Workouts in the Off-Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-build-zone-based-workouts-in-the-off-season/)
- Block/mini-block structure for ultra durability (Molly Seidel, coach Cliff Pittman, 2026): high volume of Zone 2 with controlled Zone 3 in long runs; over a 7-10 day window, 2-3 higher-volume/key aerobic sessions followed by 1-2 days reduced volume; "The higher the intensity of training, the more polarized the week must be, with very clear separation between hard efforts and recovery"; mini-block intensity must be low enough to repeat on consecutive days; "these blocks must be followed by genuine recovery"; monitored via Pa:HR cardiac drift and NGP — [How to Build Fatigue Resistance for Ultras](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/fatigue-resistance-ultras-molly-seidel-training/)
- Durability research (described as "relatively limited"): some relationship between durability and time below aerobic threshold (VT1/LT1) and polarized distribution, but the strongest predictor is years of accumulated training, which is why older athletes tend to be more durable — [Predicting and Measuring Kilojoules to Determine Athlete Durability](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/predicting-measuring-kilojoules-trainingpeaks/)
- Recovery between hard sessions (track/college runners): early season with aerobic focus, workouts may be spaced with one day or less recovery and up to three aerobic-intensity workouts per week; as anaerobic interval volume rises, "two or three recovery days will be needed"; anaerobic intervals use full recovery of 100-200% of interval time — [Training for Track Distance Events](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/training-for-track-distance-events/)
- Hill-sprint dosing for triathlon run: 1-2 sessions/week spaced 48-72 h apart, under 45 min, 5-8% grade, max-explosive 8-15 s efforts with 30-60 s rest — [How to Run Faster in an Olympic-Distance Triathlon](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-run-faster-in-an-olympic-distance-triathlon/)
- HRV-based recovery time after sessions (Stanley et al. review of eight studies): parasympathetic recovery needs <24 h after low intensity (zones 1-2), 24-48 h after threshold work (zones 3-4), >48 h after high-intensity (HIIT, zones 5-6); intensity has the largest effect on perceived training stress — [The Whole Picture: Physical Stress](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-physical-stress/)
- Immune "open window": markers of immune function fall for about 24-48 h after intense efforts of more than several hours — [What You Need to Know About Endurance Training and COVID Immunity](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-you-need-to-know-about-endurance-training-and-coivd-immunity/)
- Swim frequency for age-group triathletes: "sweet spot ... around three or four sessions per week during the season"; a swim-focus block can raise swim volume 50-100%+ but must be built gradually to protect shoulders/elbows — [5 Must-Do Off-Season Triathlon Swim Training Tips](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/5-must-do-off-season-triathlon-swim-training-tips/)
- Ultra volume progression: coach Stef Flippin wants an athlete "comfortably running 60 to 70 minutes a day without overt fatigue or nagging soreness before adding in doubles"; she programs injury-prone and masters ultrarunners in 9-10 day "weeks" targeting time on feet rather than mileage — [Ultramarathon Strength and Mobility Training Technique](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/ultramarathon-strength-and-mobility-training-technique/)
- Rapid load changes cut both ways: rapid increases raise illness/injury risk, but rapid declines over 4-6 weeks reduce resilience and raise injury risk when previous loads are restored; a steady rise in CTL builds resilience; TSB and acute:chronic ratio are good monitors of a safe rate — [The Whole Picture: Physical Stress](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-physical-stress/)
- Spring-season warning: a "sudden and jarring ramp rate increase puts you at risk of overtraining and non-functional overreaching or, at worst, injury"; shifting from strength to strength-endurance/plyometrics adds intensity that the PMC does not capture, so let strength adaptation settle before adding volume — [Staying Injury-Free through Transition Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/staying-injury-free-through-transition-season/)
- Detraining tolerance (supports planned breaks): ~15 days inactivity for a 4% VO2max drop in well-trained runners; ~3 weeks for a significant anaerobic threshold reduction; at least 12 days of zero training before performance decrements; strength/lean mass losses only after 3+ weeks; on vacation keep any exercise to RPE <= 5/10 — [Vacations: Remarkably Valuable for Athlete Fitness](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/vacations-valuable-athlete-fitness/)
- Taper: masters coach starts tapering mileage two weeks out and avoids "catch-up" training; taper compliance matters in both directions (athletes who skip sessions and athletes who add efforts) — [The Art of Coaching 60+ Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-athletes-in-60s-endurance-sports/); [Rethinking the Taper Phase](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/rethinking-the-taper-phase/)
- Off-season length: professionals cited range from no time off to up to six weeks off structured training; "Taking a full-length offseason and not being afraid to de-train a little" is recommended — [Determining When Your Athletes Are Prepared to Train Again](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/determining-when-athletes-train-again/)
- Athlete profile matters for interval prescription: speed-dominant athletes tolerate short intervals (e.g., 30 s on/30 s off) better than 2-4 min VO2 blocks; endurance-dominant athletes need longer threshold reps (mile-2 km vs 800 m) — [Speed v. Endurance](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/speed-v-endurance/)

### Inferences
- For a log-review assistant, the category supports a rule set in load terms rather than zone-percentage terms: flag weeks where CTL rises more than ~7 points, TSB falls below about -30, or weekly load jumps more than ~10%; flag absence of a lighter week within any 4-week window.
- A defensible "consecutive hard days" rule can be derived from the HRV recovery windows: threshold sessions should normally be separated by >=24-48 h and VO2/HIIT sessions by >48 h (~2 days); two HIIT sessions on consecutive days is a risk pattern. Masters guidance (9-10 day cycle, "most can't put together back-to-back hard days anymore") supports stricter spacing for athletes over ~50.
- Combining the RPE rule (90% of sessions RPE 5-7) and the research example (~65/25/10 by time) suggests flagging weeks where more than ~10-15% of training time or more than ~2 sessions per 7 days are high-intensity, and a time-limited athlete (5-12 h/week) should see most of their TSS coming from Zone 2/low Zone 3 with 1-2 quality sessions.
- Volume vs intensity for 5-12 h/week athletes: the category's own numbers (6-8 h/week producing CTL ~60; 8.25 h/week for a regional cyclocross winner; ~9 h/week in the cycling study) suggest that a well-structured 6-9 h week is competitive at age-group level, and that going beyond ~12 h mainly changes how many long sessions and doubles can be absorbed, not the intensity share.

### Gaps
- No Science-category article states polarized vs pyramidal recommendations, an 80/20 rule, or whether to count by sessions or time; no article gives session-count guidance per week for triathletes beyond the swim (3-4/week). These would need sources outside the category.
- No article gives a numeric definition of a "recovery week" (e.g., percentage load reduction) or of long-session frequency (e.g., one long ride and one long run per week).
- No article directly contrasts plans for 5-12 h/week athletes against higher-volume athletes; the inferences above are assembled from separate pieces.

## Key question 2: What do the articles say about HRV and resting HR interpretation (baseline, 7-day rolling, when to back off)?

### Takeaway
Measure HRV (RMSSD, log-transformed) every morning in a consistent seated/standing position, compare the day to a 7-10-day rolling baseline, and act on the trend rather than the daily value: a 5-10% baseline dip during an intensified block that rebounds in the recovery week is normal, a baseline that stays out of range despite reduced load signals non-functional overreaching, and a resting HR ~5 bpm above average means more recovery is needed (>20 bpm is a red-flag for cardiac review after illness).

### Cited Findings
- Measurement protocol: 1-minute test on waking, 4-7 mornings/week (elsewhere 4-5 minimum), validated chest strap or finger sensor; wrist-based optical sensors are "not yet considered accurate enough" for HRV; higher than personal baseline/moving average = recovered, lower = stress/compromised recovery — [The Coach's Guide to HRV Monitoring](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-coachs-guide-to-hrv-monitoring/); [Best Practices for Taking HRV Readings](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/best-practices-for-taking-hrv-readings/)
- Position: supine scores ~3-5 points higher than sitting, sitting ~3-5 higher than standing; stand if sitting resting HR is mid-50s or lower (parasympathetic saturation); lying/night-time readings "are not suitable for athletes" for the same reason — [What Factors Can Generate a Good (or Bad) HRV Score](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-constitutes-a-good-or-bad-hrv-score/); [Best Practices for Taking HRV Readings](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/best-practices-for-taking-hrv-readings/); [Explaining HRV Numbers and Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/explaining-hrv-numbers-age/)
- Baseline: best practice is a rolling 7-10-day average using RMSSD (RMSSD-guided training outperformed frequency-domain HF in the meta-analysis); daily changes of ~10% are normal, "like body weight"; establish the baseline in a light-load or off-season period; one coach suggests tracking for three months before using HRV for decisions — [Is HRV Training the Best Approach for Athletes?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hrv-guided-training/); [Explaining HRV Numbers and Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/explaining-hrv-numbers-age/); [Best Practices for Taking HRV Readings](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/best-practices-for-taking-hrv-readings/); [Determining When Your Athletes Are Prepared to Train Again](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/determining-when-athletes-train-again/)
- Daily decision rule: if morning HRV is close to or above baseline, continue planned training; if significantly below, do lower intensity or complete rest "depending on how long the dip has continued" — [Is HRV Training the Best Approach for Athletes?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hrv-guided-training/)
- 7-day trend trigger (from the cycling study): "pile on the load until the HRV 7-day average starts to decline, then back off either with rest or low-intensity sessions until the HRV 7-day average rebounds"; a practical threshold is a 1.5-point drop in 20xLnRMSSD (~7.5% drop in raw RMSSD), or 0.5 SD of the 7-day average as the smallest worthwhile change; example athlete's ATL:CTL ratio approached 1.6 as the 7-day HRV fell from 105 to 102 before rebounding above the start after two reduced-TSS days; "Don't train hard when sick"; HRV-guided training beat the fixed plan (peak power +5%, VT2 power +14%, 40-min TT +7%) but the author cautions wider adoption is needed to know if it applies to recreational and elite athletes — [New Study Widens HRV Evidence for More Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/new-study-widens-hrv-evidence-for-more-athletes/)
- Meta-analysis context (10 small studies, all under 8 weeks): "modest effects in favor of HRV-guided training" for VO2max, VT2 and endurance performance; described as short-term and small-scale — [Is HRV Training the Best Approach for Athletes?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hrv-guided-training/)
- Expected phase patterns: intensified blocks lower HRV by ~5-10% and it should "bounce back quickly" in the block's de-loading week; high-volume base raises HRV ~5% with a small RHR drop; in a taper HRV rises, then plateaus/declines slightly with a small RHR rise in the last 3-5 days (normal, "primed"); race-day low HRV/high RHR reflects nerves and is not meaningful — [What Factors Can Generate a Good (or Bad) HRV Score](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-constitutes-a-good-or-bad-hrv-score/); [The Coach's Guide to HRV Monitoring](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-coachs-guide-to-hrv-monitoring/)
- Reading HRV with RHR: first look at a timeline of HRV, RHR and training; healthy pattern = high-for-age HRV baseline (example 85-90), low RHR (low-mid 50s), little day-to-day variation, a slowly widening HRV-RHR gap; concerning pattern = lower baseline (70-75), higher/variable RHR (50-65), frequent dips after hard days, a narrowing HRV-RHR gap, and high daily variation "suggesting that only marginal changes in total load cause a significant impact" — [What Factors Can Generate a Good (or Bad) HRV Score](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-constitutes-a-good-or-bad-hrv-score/)
- Paradoxical high HRV: continuous intense training can push past low HRV into an exhausted state with "much higher HRV and lower resting HR than usual"; HRV "out of an athlete's normal range (both high as well as low) despite reduced training loads" is a research sign of NFOR — [The Coach's Guide to HRV Monitoring](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-coachs-guide-to-hrv-monitoring/); [New Study Widens HRV Evidence](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/new-study-widens-hrv-evidence-for-more-athletes/)
- Illness: HRV falls "rapidly" at illness onset, RHR rose above normal only on day three in the example; HRV is the faster indicator; the athlete resumed light training on day six for three days; a "large decline in daily HRV" means rest — [The Coach's Guide to HRV Monitoring](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-coachs-guide-to-hrv-monitoring/); [New Study Widens HRV Evidence](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/new-study-widens-hrv-evidence-for-more-athletes/)
- Resting HR thresholds: elevated RHR after a hard day is normal; "Usually, an RHR 5 bpm above their average indicates they need more recovery time"; RHR elevated for multiple weeks suggests overtraining or illness — [The 4 Signs of Overtraining](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- RHR norms and caveats: athletes commonly 40-60 bpm; WHOOP averages 55.2 (men) and 58.8 (women); a stable 75 bpm is "probably" not a concern, but upward drift or seismograph-like spikes warrant a look at load/lifestyle and a physician if worried; post-exercise HR recovery <12 bpm in the first minute was linked to higher mortality (NEJM) and merits cardiology referral; falling cardiovascular capacity through a program may indicate overtraining/under-recovery — [Does it Matter if Your Athletes Have High Resting Heart Rates?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/does-it-matter-if-your-athletes-have-high-resting-and-working-heart-rates/)
- Post-COVID red flag: resting HR increase >20 bpm, palpitations, chest pain, breathlessness at rest, fainting, or marked fitness loss = stop and refer to a physician; daily questionnaire for 10+ days — [Returning to Training and Racing After COVID-19](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/returning-to-training-and-racing-after-covid-19/)
- Season-end fatigue signal: an athlete's lowest and highest HR values "increasingly come closer together" — [Determining When Your Athletes Are Prepared to Train Again](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/determining-when-athletes-train-again/)
- Age norms (general population, Lifelines cohort 150,000+): RMSSD declines from ~80 ms (teens) to ~25 ms (75+), men and women similar; average at age 40 ~65; endurance athletes often >=80; raw scores 60-70 = healthy, 90-100+ = top endurance athletes, 40-55 = less healthy, below that a concern; masters athletes (mean age 51) had HRV equal to active 25-year-olds; HRV baseline changes correlate with and may predict performance changes after a block — [Explaining HRV Numbers and Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/explaining-hrv-numbers-age/); [What Factors Can Generate a Good (or Bad) HRV Score](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-constitutes-a-good-or-bad-hrv-score/)
- Menstrual cycle: HRV (RMSSD, SDNN) is higher pre-ovulation and falls after, with a parallel RHR rise; a 2019 study of seven cyclists found lower HRV 4-11 days after ovulation; the 2020 meta-analysis (78 studies, many low quality) found a small, consistent performance reduction in the early follicular phase and high individual variation; implications are described as "not as clear as we would like" — [HRV, Athlete Performance and the Menstrual Cycle](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/hrv-performance-and-the-menstrual-cycle/)
- HRV as total-load meter: HRV reflects sleep, nutrition, mental stress, alcohol, illness and inflammation, not just training; athletes with disorganized lifestyles have lower, less consistent baselines; HRV is more sensitive and earlier than RHR because it isolates vagal activity while RHR mixes sympathetic, parasympathetic and hormonal influences — [What Heart Rate Variability Reveals About Your Health](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/what-heart-rate-variability-reveals-about-your-health/); [The Whole Picture: An Introduction to Total Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-an-introduction-to-total-load/)
- Context is required: record subjective fatigue, soreness, mood, sleep, stress, illness, medication, travel and cycle dates alongside HRV, and display TSB/ACWR next to it; do not measure on race day; use paced breathing (7-10 breaths/min) — [Best Practices for Taking HRV Readings](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/best-practices-for-taking-hrv-readings/); [Explaining HRV Numbers and Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/explaining-hrv-numbers-age/)

### Inferences
- A log-review rule set consistent with these articles: (a) compute a 7-day rolling mean of ln-RMSSD; (b) "amber" when the 7-day mean drops >0.5 SD or ~7.5% below the athlete's baseline during a build -> replace the next high-intensity session with low intensity; (c) "red" when the 7-day mean stays outside the normal band for more than a week despite a lighter week, or when HRV is abnormally HIGH with lower RHR after sustained heavy training; (d) RHR +5 bpm above 30-day average -> add recovery; RHR elevated for 2+ weeks -> consider illness/OTS; RHR +20 bpm after infection -> stop and refer.
- Night-time wearable HRV (Garmin, Oura, Whoop) is treated by these authors as less sensitive for trained athletes (saturation); an assistant should weight it lower than a morning seated/standing reading, or at least use only its trend.

### Gaps
- No article gives a validated numeric HRV "red line" other than the 0.5 SD / 7.5% trigger from one small study; no article specifies how many consecutive low days should force a full rest day.
- The evidence for HRV-guided training is explicitly described as short-term, small-sample, and mostly in trained cyclists/runners; not established for recreational or elite athletes.
- Author conflict of interest: most HRV articles are by the ithlete founder.

## Key question 3: What are the flagged signs of overtraining and how many recovery weeks per block?

### Takeaway
The articles distinguish functional overreaching (performance dips then supercompensates after a short recovery), non-functional overreaching (weeks of no gain; HRV/RHR stay abnormal despite rest) and overtraining/OTS (weeks to months, >2 months performance decrement). Flagged signs: persistently elevated RHR, suppressed or abnormally high HRV despite rest, performance plateau near the athlete's CTL ceiling (~110 for amateurs), rising RPE for the same output, mood/motivation loss, sleep and appetite disruption, frequent illness, and compulsive training or underfueling. The only explicit recovery-week cadence in the category is one recovery week every third or fourth week.

### Cited Findings
- Definitions: overreaching = performance drop from acute heavy load, "resolved in a few days"; overtraining = response to chronic heavy load, "can take a few weeks to months to restore" — [The 4 Signs of Overtraining](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- Four signs: (1) RHR elevated for an extended period (+5 bpm above average = need recovery; weeks = overtrained/ill); (2) reduced red cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit (blood tests at least twice a year, compare to own baseline); (3) HRV lower than usual for a longer period despite recovery days; (4) training plateau despite hard training; amateurs' plateau often near CTL ~110; "An athlete should not train hard year-round without taking regular periods of reduced volume and intensity" — [The 4 Signs of Overtraining](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-4-signs-of-overtraining/)
- FOR vs NFOR: FOR occurs when planned overload plus recovery yields a performance increase; NFOR occurs when training is "too intense, too extended, or both": recovery takes much longer and no gains result — [The Whole Picture: Physical Stress](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-physical-stress/)
- NFOR detection with HRV: in a recreational-runner overload study, HRV differed little at the end of overload but the recovery trajectory differed sharply between acutely-fatigued (kept improving) and functionally-overreached runners (small improvement); FOR runners also had significantly worse subjective physical scores at end of overload; coaches should log wellness/fatigue especially when TSB and ACWR worsen — [Functional Overreaching vs. Excess Fatigue: How HRV Can Help](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fine-line-between-functional-training-and-overreaching/)
- Vuelta example: riders' HRV averaged 55 ms early, one domestique fell to ~17 ms by the end ("comparable to a heart attack patient") — [Functional Overreaching vs. Excess Fatigue](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-fine-line-between-functional-training-and-overreaching/)
- OTS duration: overtraining "results in a prolonged performance decrement (>two months) and more severe symptoms" (ACSM); compulsive exercise driven by endorphin/dopamine reward leads to intense sessions without recovery; among 202 female endurance athletes training >=5 days/week, 23% tended to overtrain, 21% at risk of disordered eating, 65% at risk of low energy availability; treat obsessive overtraining as "a major red flag" and check eating patterns alongside training logs — [How Training Addiction and Disordered Eating are Connected](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/training-addiction-disordered-eating-connected/)
- NFOR symptom list (Bubbs): fatigue, weakness and poor performance that "linger, and you don't bounce back after rest", low energy, more muscle ache, heavier-feeling weights; overtraining edge: declines in performance and mood, hormonal disturbances, frequent sickness, sleep and appetite disruption — [Vacations: Remarkably Valuable for Athlete Fitness](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/vacations-valuable-athlete-fitness/)
- RPE drift as an early signal: if an athlete logs a tempo or threshold session as harder than usual and paces/intensity slip, poor sleep is a likely culprit; sleep loss raised RPE, reduced time to exhaustion ~11% at 80% VO2max, raised HR for the same power, and lowered 12-min run distance after one night of 4 h sleep — [Revealing the Impact of Sleep Loss on Athletes' Performance Physiology](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/impact-sleep-loss-performance-physiology/)
- Mood and motivation are "two of the best indicators for readiness to train"; a day or two off may reset them; the "non-negotiables" before a heavy block are nutrition, sleep, stress and movement quality; pull training back when life "bucket" overflows — [When Does Recovery Become More Important Than Training?](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/when-does-recovery-become-more-important-than-training/)
- Burnout signs: exhaustion, cynicism/negativity, sense of ineffectiveness; in athletes: skipping or non-compliance, frustration with the plan, inability to set goals or be satisfied with results, plus body aches, headaches, sleep disturbance and weakened immunity; causes include overtraining, returning too fast after a race, over-eager race sign-ups and insufficient sleep — [Understanding How Athlete Burnout Happens](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/understanding-burnout-how-prevent/)
- Mental stress as load: in a 2-week intensive program, highly stressed adults gained almost no max cycling power vs up to 19% for the least stressed; in 30 triathletes over a season, increases in psychological stress had the largest impact on illness, injury and burnout — [The Whole Picture: Mental Stress](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-mental-stress/)
- Life stress delays recovery: high life-event stress impaired muscle-function recovery and increased pain, fatigue and soreness for up to four days after a hard session; chronic underfueling can convert a tolerable program into OTS; IOC suggests +300-600 kcal/day to correct low energy availability — [Beware of These Lifestyle Factors Adding to an Athlete's Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/lifestyle-factors-adding-training-load/)
- Daily readiness survey (1-5): sleep, soreness, stress, energy, mood, social engagement; track averages and per-item trends — [Tracking Psychological Health In TrainingPeaks](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/tracking-psychological-health-in-trainingpeaks/)
- Illness/injury rule: reduce training stress for 24 h for a non-sinister pain; if pain lasts >48 h refer to a medical professional; rearrange hard days rather than only cutting volume — [A Coach's Guide to Injury Prevention](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/a-coachs-guide-to-injury-prevention/)
- Post-illness return (COVID-specific, Jan 2021 and Jul 2022 guidance): 10 days no exercise for asymptomatic or mild cases and only restart when "well", moderate illness = isolation until 24 h fever-free then 10 days no exercise, myocarditis = 3-6 months restriction; return "takes weeks or months, not days"; 1-3% of screened pro athletes developed myocarditis — [Returning to Training and Racing After COVID-19](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/returning-to-training-and-racing-after-covid-19/); [Return to Play Protocols for Post-COVID-19 Infections](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/covid-19-return-to-play-protocols/)
- Recovery-week cadence: the ATP "recovery cycle (every third or fourth week)" — [How To Design An Endurance Athlete's Total Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/designing-an-athletes-total-load/); one female-focused coach aligns the recovery week with menstruation and adds an extra rest day or low-intensity drills that week — [How Menstrual Cycle Tracking Can Revolutionize Female Athlete Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/reasons-coaches-should-know-menstrual-cycle/)
- Rest days for perimenopausal athletes: "a consistent day off, or even two to three rest days a week, may be necessary" — [Coaching Female Athletes with Perimenopause and Menopause](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coach-female-athletes-perimenopause-menopause/)
- Sleep as the primary recovery lever: ~five 90-minute cycles per night on average across the week, deficits can be repaid with extended sleep or 20-30-min naps; swimmers and triathletes have the earliest rise times and poorest sleep efficiency in AIS surveys — [The Whole Picture: Recovery Through Sleep](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-recovery-through-sleep/)
- IOC load consensus: injury and illness risk rises with loads that are too low, too high, or that change rapidly; monitor internal and external load, load consistency, psychological stress, travel and illness — [The Whole Picture: An Introduction to Total Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-whole-picture-an-introduction-to-total-load/)

### Inferences
- A pattern-based warning list for a log reviewer: (1) RPE rising for equal pace/power across 2+ sessions; (2) RHR >= +5 bpm for 3+ days or elevated 2+ weeks; (3) 7-day HRV below band despite a lighter week; (4) missed or shortened sessions plus negative comments (motivation/mood); (5) no down-week in 4+ weeks while CTL keeps rising or TSB stays below -30; (6) reported poor sleep with harder-than-planned sessions; (7) training volume rising while body weight/fueling notes fall (LEA risk).
- "Recovery week every 3rd or 4th week" combined with the FOR/NFOR framing implies a block of 2-3 loading weeks followed by 1 unloading week, with the older-athlete guidance shifting toward 2:1 rather than 3:1.

### Gaps
- No article quantifies how much to reduce load in a recovery week, or how many days of complete rest per week are appropriate for general age-group athletes (only the perimenopause and masters pieces give rest-day numbers).
- No article provides a validated overtraining questionnaire (e.g., POMS, RESTQ) or scoring thresholds; the readiness survey is the author's own.

## Key question 4: What do the articles say about strength training and about ageing athletes' recovery needs?

### Takeaway
Strength work is presented as a year-round requirement for injury resilience (bone, tendon, connective tissue), economy and power: build with heavy, low-rep lifting 2-3x/week (3-4 sets of 5-8, or 3-6 sets of 3-5 for experienced lifters, at RPE 7-8, never 1RM), maintain in-season at 1-2x/week, keep plyometrics small (30-45 contacts, 2-3x/week) and place power work first with up to 72 h between power sessions. For athletes over ~40-50 the articles prescribe more recovery (9-10-day cycles, no back-to-back hard days, more cross-training and easy sessions), at least two strength sessions a week, higher protein (~1.2-2.0 g/kg/day, ~30 g per meal), and greater attention to sleep and hydration; masters' HRV can equal that of active 25-year-olds, but VO2max falls ~10% per decade after 50 and muscle mass ~1%/year.

### Cited Findings
Strength dosing and periodization
- Stability first (15-20 min, 1-3 days/week, up to three months for novices, then weekly), then strength: 3-4 sets of 5-8 reps, heavy, controlled; build 2-3x/week, maintain 1-2x/week when sport-specific volume rises; plyometrics 30-45 reps 2-3x/week, box no higher than mid-shin initially, progress by shorter ground contact not height; during taper keep intensity but cut volume (one set of 5-8, or 30% of normal plyometric work) — [The 3 Most Important Components of Strength Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/the-three-most-important-parts-of-strength-training/)
- Three session types: power = 3-5 sets x 3-5 reps, maximally explosive, early in the workout, CNS may need up to 72 h (Monday -> Thursday); strength = fundamental movements (hinge, carry, push, pull, squat), 3-6 reps heavy, 2-3 sets, stop before fatigue, 2-3 days/week; muscular endurance = 10-15 s hard / 45-60 s rest repeats, or long intervals with rest until HR <= 120 bpm — [3 Types of Resistance Training Sessions Endurance Athletes Need](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/3-types-resistance-training-athletes-need/)
- Evidence for tissue durability: bone remodels to mechanical load (Wolff's law) and endurance work alone does not load bone enough; heavy slow resistance and eccentric training both improved Achilles tendinopathy (RCT) and HSR improved patellar tendinopathy in 12 weeks; recommend 2-3 sessions/week; eccentric-emphasis work at 50-65% of normal load; connective tissue adapts more slowly than muscle — [The Latest Evidence Proving Resistance Training is Essential](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/evidence-proving-resistance-training-essential/)
- Plyometrics improved running economy and time trials from 1 km to 40 min via motor-unit recruitment, tendon stiffness and coordination; the author notes further research is needed and a lack of studies in females and youth — [Strength Training, Plyometrics & Running Economy](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/strength-training-plyometrics-running-economy/)
- Do not drop strength before races and do not switch to bodyweight-only mid-season; beginners: 2-3 sets at RPE 5-7, 2-3 days/week, 3-5 min rest between sets; experienced: 1-2 days/week of 3-6 sets x 3-5 reps of 1-2 "Fundamental 5+1" movements in early-mid build; a session should take no more than 65 minutes and leave the athlete fresh for swim/bike/run — [Transitioning Strength Training for the Triathlon Racing Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/transitioning-strength-training-for-triathlon-racing-season/)
- Weekly placement example: Mon movement session, Tue heavy, Wed no strength (key endurance/tempo day), Thu moderate paired with the athlete's strongest sport; "works well for 70% of the athletes" — [How to Run Faster in an Olympic-Distance Triathlon](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-run-faster-in-an-olympic-distance-triathlon/)
- Program balance: Fundamental 5+1 = push, pull, squat, hinge, press, rotary stability; lower-body-only programs create imbalances — [How Endurance Strength Training Programs Go Wrong](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/where-endurance-strength-training-programs-often-go-wrong/)
- Mid-season start: two quality sessions/week, <=35 min each, first two weeks light ("anatomical adaptation"), weights at RPE 5-6 — [Using Strength Training to Salvage a Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/using-strength-training-to-salvage-a-season/)
- Beginner scheduling to avoid DOMS: no strength on long-run/long-ride days; lower-body strength on swim days, upper-body on easy bike/run days; consistency lowers injury; CrossFit-style Olympic lifting carries higher shoulder/spine injury risk. Note this author (Sankoff) advocates lighter loads and more reps for triathletes, which CONTRADICTS the heavy/low-rep advice of Sapp and Brodie — [Introducing Strength Training to Beginner Triathletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/introducing-strength-training-to-beginner-triathletes/) vs [Why Strength Training Becomes More Important as We Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/why-strength-training-becomes-more-important-as-we-age/)
- Ultrarunners: 1-3 gym sessions/week combining resistance and plyometrics; masters/injury-prone ultrarunners programmed in 9-10-day weeks — [Ultramarathon Strength and Mobility Training Technique](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/ultramarathon-strength-and-mobility-training-technique/)
- Strength load is invisible to TSS/PMC; count it in total-load judgments and let strength adaptations settle before piling on volume — [How To Design An Endurance Athlete's Total Training Load](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/designing-an-athletes-total-load/); [Staying Injury-Free through Transition Season](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/staying-injury-free-through-transition-season/)
- Velocity-based training (2025) offers autoregulated load without 1RM testing — [Velocity-Based Strength Training](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/velocity-based-strength-training-a-smarter-approach-to-building-power/)

Ageing athletes
- After 40, tissue circulation capacity falls 40-60%, slowing repair and lengthening recovery; speedwork and hills are more taxing with age, so heavy lifting (3-4 x 5-8) is the preferred strength stimulus; "with any athlete over the age of 40, strength training is a must"; lift 2-3x/week early season, 1-2x/week to maintain — [Why Strength Training Becomes More Important as We Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/why-strength-training-becomes-more-important-as-we-age/)
- Masters mistakes: strength only in off-season (strength/explosiveness drop in as little as two weeks), not training heavy (RPE 7-8, sets of 3-4, but no 1RM), plyometrics without a strength base, mimicking sport movements (<15% of good programs match the sport, ~40% counter it) — [4 Common Strength Training Mistakes Masters Athletes Make](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/4-common-strength-training-mistakes-for-masters-and-how-to-avoid-them/)
- 60+ coaching (Lawrence Herrera): VO2max falls ~10% per decade after 50; muscle mass down ~12% by 60-70 and 30% by 80; at least two strength sessions/week, 3:1 pull-to-push ratio, off-season hypertrophy block; "Recovery is even more important as endurance athletes age. Unless they're elite, most can't put together back-to-back hard days anymore"; more cross-training/recovery rides; uses Joe Friel's nine-day cycle so an athlete does ~6 hard interval sessions a month instead of 8; swaps a demanding session for lower intensity when sleep/soreness/fatigue are poor; taper starts two weeks out; older athletes under-eat protein (endurance athletes need ~20%+ more than sedentary people, older athletes more still) and under-drink (thirst declines; ~1 oz water per kg bodyweight baseline) — [The Art of Coaching 60+ Athletes in Endurance Sports](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coaching-athletes-in-60s-endurance-sports/)
- Masters nutrition: "masters" begins at 35; sarcopenia ~1%/year mass and ~3%/year strength; protein 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day (~50% more on heavy/strength days; 1.5 g/kg may be needed), ~30 g per meal and 10-20 g per snack; creatine 3-5 g/day; omega-3 3-5 g/day; measure sweat loss because thirst perception declines; beta-blockers lower HR and can mask hypoglycemia — [Addressing the Changing Nutrition Needs of Masters Athletes](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/addressing-nutrition-needs-masters-athletes/)
- Sleep in older athletes: "many modestly older athletes just can't" live with less sleep, including 40-year-olds with full lives; testosterone release depends on sleep; routine bedtime, alarm 20 min before, gear laid out — [How to Coach Older Athletes to Get Better Sleep](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/how-to-coach-older-athletes-to-get-better-sleep/)
- Perimenopause/menopause (average onset 45 and 51): declining estrogen makes lean mass harder to keep, so specific strength sessions are essential; HIIT/SIT (10-30 s max efforts) 2-3 sessions in off-season, 1-2 in base/build, 1 in race prep; plyometrics 30-60 s to start for bone density (up to one-fifth of bone density can be lost in perimenopause); longer warm-ups, soft-tissue work; sleep disruption from falling progesterone means "a consistent day off, or even two to three rest days a week, may be necessary" — [Coaching Female Athletes with Perimenopause and Menopause](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/coach-female-athletes-perimenopause-menopause/)
- Masters HRV: masters athletes averaging age 51 had HRV as high as active 25-year-olds; HRV declines exponentially with age in the general population; masters athletes are the most frequent patients in sports cardiology clinics (relevant to post-illness caution) — [Explaining HRV Numbers and Age](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/explaining-hrv-numbers-age/); [Returning to Training and Racing After COVID-19](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/returning-to-training-and-racing-after-covid-19/)
- Durability improves with training years, so older athletes are often more durable despite lower ceilings — [Predicting and Measuring Kilojoules to Determine Athlete Durability](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/coach-blog/predicting-measuring-kilojoules-trainingpeaks/)

### Inferences
- For a log reviewer: for athletes >= ~45-50, evaluate hard-session spacing on a 9-10-day window (target ~2 quality sessions per 9-10 days, i.e., ~6 per month), flag back-to-back hard days, and expect 1-3 full rest days per week (more for perimenopausal athletes with poor sleep). For younger age-groupers the 7-day cycle with 2-3 quality sessions and one recovery week per 3-4 is the default.
- Strength sessions should be logged and treated as load even though TSS ignores them; a week with heavy lifting plus a threshold run within 24 h, or plyometrics on the same day as a long run, is a risk pattern per the beginner-scheduling advice.

### Gaps
- Contradiction on load: Sankoff (2021) recommends lighter weights/more reps for triathletes while Sapp (2020) and Brodie (2019) insist on heavy low-rep work; the category does not resolve this, though the majority and the cited research (HSR tendon RCTs) favour heavy work once movement quality is established.
- No article quantifies how much extra recovery (in hours/days) an athlete in their 40s vs 60s needs after a given session type, nor how to adjust CTL ramp rate by age.
- Evidence on plyometrics is flagged by the author as needing more research, especially in females; masters supplement evidence is noted as thinner than in younger athletes.
